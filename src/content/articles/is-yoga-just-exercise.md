Search for "yoga" today and you'll mostly see bodies: flexible, toned, photographed mid-pose on a beach. It's no surprise that many people think of yoga as a kind of exercise, somewhere between stretching and Pilates.

The short answer is: **modern posture classes are exercise, and yoga is much more than that.** Here's how the two became confused.

## What yoga was before it was a workout

For most of its history, yoga meant disciplines of the mind and spirit. Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras* define yoga as the stilling of the mind and give postures three short verses. The *Bhagavad Gita* describes yoga as evenness of mind and skill in action. Medieval hatha yoga did develop postures, breath practices and cleansing methods, but as preparation for meditation and higher states, not as fitness.

In 19th-century India, many educated Indians and British observers saw the wandering hatha yogis, with their extreme austerities, as embarrassing or suspect. When Swami Vivekananda introduced yoga to Western audiences in Chicago in 1893, and in his book *Raja Yoga* (1896), he focused on philosophy and meditation and largely dismissed the physical practices.

## How the poses took centre stage

The posture-focused yoga most people know today largely took shape in the 20th century:

- **Physical culture was booming** worldwide: gymnastics, bodybuilding and exercise systems for health and national strength. In India this mixed with a revival of interest in indigenous traditions.
- In the 1930s, **T. Krishnamacharya** taught at the Mysore palace, developing flowing sequences of postures for young students. Historians have pointed out that some of his methods resemble the gymnastics of the day as much as older hatha texts.
- His students, **B.K.S. Iyengar, K. Pattabhi Jois and Indra Devi**, took posture-based yoga to Europe and America from the 1950s onwards.
- In the West, it found a market as **health, fitness and stress relief**, and later as a lifestyle industry of clothing, studios, festivals and teacher trainings.

None of this was a conspiracy. Posture practice was easy to teach in groups, easy to see working, and easy to sell. Ethics, breath and meditation are quieter, slower and harder to photograph.

## What tends to get left out

When yoga is taught mainly as exercise, a lot of the tradition quietly drops away:

- **The ethical foundation:** the yamas and niyamas, the first two limbs.
- **The breath as practice in itself:** pranayama as a discipline, not just "breathe in, breathe out".
- **Meditation:** the actual goal of the posture practice.
- **The texts and the philosophy:** why any of this is done.
- **The idea of a teacher-student relationship** over years, rather than a drop-in class.

<div class="aside">

**A quick test for any class:** Does it give time to stillness, not just movement? Is breath taught with care? Is there any mention of why the poses are done? A class can be a great workout and still be yoga, if those threads are there.

</div>

## What the texts say a posture is for

Patanjali's teaching on posture is just this:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">स्थिरसुखमासनम्</p>
<p class="iast">sthira-sukham āsanam</p>
<p class="tr">Posture should be steady and easy.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Posture is that which is firm and pleasant.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.46</p>
</div>

The next two sutras say this comes from relaxing effort and resting the mind on the infinite, and that the result is freedom from being disturbed by opposites such as heat and cold. The point of the posture is stillness, not performance. We look at all three sutras in [what the Yoga Sutras actually say about asana](/the-texts/yoga-sutras-on-asana/).

## So, is yoga just exercise?

The way it's often sold, it can be. The way it was taught for most of its history, it never was. The good news is that the two aren't enemies: a posture practice done with steady breath and a quiet mind is already a step down the older path. You just have to know the path is there.
