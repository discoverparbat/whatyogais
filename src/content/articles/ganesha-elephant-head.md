Ganesha is honoured first at the start of almost anything in Nepal and India: a wedding, a new shop, a journey, a school year. He is the most recognisable figure in the whole tradition. And his strange, beautiful form is itself a teaching.

## The story

Shiva was away, deep in meditation in the mountains. Parvati, wanting someone of her own to guard her privacy, shaped a boy from the turmeric paste she bathed with and breathed life into him. "You are my son," she told him. "Let no one in."

Shiva came home. A boy he had never seen blocked the door and refused to let him in. Shiva's attendants fought the boy and lost. At last Shiva, in anger, cut off the boy's head.

Parvati's grief turned to fury. Unless her son lived again, she said, she would destroy the world. Shiva, realising what he had done, sent his attendants north with orders to bring the head of the first creature they found. They found an elephant. Shiva set its head on the boy's body and brought him back to life. Then, to honour him, he made him lord of his attendants, the *gaṇas*: **Gaṇapati**, **Gaṇeśa**. And he declared that the boy would be worshipped first, before any other god.

This is the best-known version, from the *Śiva Purāṇa*. The *Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa* tells a different one, in which the baby's head is lost when the planet Shani glances at him. Different regions tell it differently again. The core image is the same: a head lost, and a far greater one given.

## What the tradition reads in it

### The boy at the door is the ego

A widely shared reading sees the boy as the ego: the small, separate self that stands guard, keeps things out and insists on itself, even against Shiva, pure consciousness. He isn't evil; he's doing his duty as he understands it. But he doesn't recognise his own father.

The head must go. Not the person: the head, the narrow identity. And what replaces it isn't nothing. It's something vast, ancient and wise. That's what the tradition means by realisation: not the destruction of the person, but the small self giving way to something infinitely larger. ### His form, piece by piece

Teachers draw lessons from every part of Ganesha's form. These are traditional readings, not one fixed doctrine:

| Feature | Common reading |
|---|---|
| **large head** | think big; wisdom |
| **large ears** | listen much, to the teacher and the scriptures (*śravaṇa*) |
| **small eyes** | focused, one-pointed attention |
| **small mouth** | speak little |
| **one broken tusk** | keep the good, let go of the bad; also the tusk he is said to have used to write down the Mahabharata |
| **large belly** | digest all of life's experiences, good and bad, calmly |
| **the mouse** | desire, small and restless, kept under control and made to serve |
| **trunk** | strong enough to uproot a tree, delicate enough to pick up a needle: a discerning mind |

### The mouse

The mouse beneath the elephant is especially loved. Desire, like a mouse, is small, quick, and gnaws at everything it can reach. Ganesha doesn't kill it. He rides it. Desire isn't destroyed but put in its place: carrying wisdom, not steering it.

### Remover of obstacles, and of the first obstacle

Ganesha is **Vighneśvara**, lord of obstacles, both their remover and, when needed, their placer. That's why he comes first. And the deepest obstacle, the tradition says, is the one the story shows at the start: the small self standing at the door.

## The Nepal connection

Ganesha is everywhere in the Kathmandu Valley, at every crossroads and courtyard. Kathmandu's **Ashok Binayak**, the small Ganesh shrine at Maru, is one of the most visited in the city. In Chobhar, Jal Binayak sits above the Bagmati gorge, and in Bhaktapur, Surya Binayak greets the morning sun. Before almost any new beginning, a Nepali family will stop at one of them.

## If you only remember one thing

The boy at the door was the small self, guarding against its own source. He lost his head, and gained a vast one. That is what the tradition hopes for us.
