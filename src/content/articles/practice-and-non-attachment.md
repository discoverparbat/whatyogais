Patanjali defines yoga as stilling the movements of the mind (1.2). The obvious next question is: how? His answer, ten sutras later, is short enough to remember for life.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">अभ्यासवैराग्याभ्यां तन्निरोधः</p>
<p class="iast">abhyāsa-vairāgyābhyāṃ tan-nirodhaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">They are stilled by practice and non-attachment.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Their control is by practice and non-attachment.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.12</p>
</div>

Two means, like two wings. Practice without non-attachment becomes grim effort that keeps getting undone by desire. Non-attachment without practice is a nice idea that never takes root. The *Bhagavad Gita* gives the same pair when Arjuna complains that the mind is as hard to hold as the wind: it can be held, Krishna answers, by practice and dispassion (6.35).

## Abhyāsa: practice

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तत्र स्थितौ यत्नोऽभ्यासः</p>
<p class="iast">tatra sthitau yatno 'bhyāsaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Practice is the effort to remain steady in that stillness.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Continuous struggle to keep them (the Vrttis) perfectly restrained is practice.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.13</p>
</div>

Practice isn't a technique. It's the repeated return. Every time the mind wanders and you bring it back, that's abhyāsa.

### Why practice works: character is habit

Vivekananda's commentary here is some of the most practical writing in his whole book. Each action, he says, is like a vibration on the surface of the lake. It dies away, but it leaves a mark, an impression (*saṃskāra*). Enough marks together become a habit. And habits are not just second nature, he says: they are the whole of what we are.

That sounds discouraging, until you see the other side. If we are made of habits, we can be remade:

<div class="aside">

“The only remedy for bad habits is counter habits... Never say any man is hopeless, because he only represents a character, a bundle of habits, and these can be checked by new and better ones. Character is repeated habits, and repeated habits alone can reform character.”

— Swami Vivekananda

</div>

That's the whole case for a daily practice. Not one great effort, but small ones repeated until they become who you are.

### How practice becomes firm

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">स तु दीर्घकालनैरन्तर्यसत्कारासेवितो दृढभूमिः</p>
<p class="iast">sa tu dīrgha-kāla-nairantarya-satkārāsevito dṛḍha-bhūmiḥ</p>
<p class="tr">It becomes firmly grounded when kept up for a long time, without break, and with devotion.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Its ground becomes firm by long, constant efforts with great love (for the end to be attained).”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.14</p>
</div>

Three conditions:

- **Long time** (*dīrgha-kāla*): months and years, not a weekend workshop
- **Without break** (*nairantarya*): daily, even when it's short
- **With devotion** (*satkāra*): with love and respect for what you're doing, not as a chore

Vivekananda's comment is one line: "Restraint does not come in one day, but by long continued practice."

## Vairāgya: non-attachment

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">दृष्टानुश्रविकविषयवितृष्णस्य वशीकारसंज्ञा वैराग्यम्</p>
<p class="iast">dṛṣṭānuśravika-viṣaya-vitṛṣṇasya vaśīkāra-saṃjñā vairāgyam</p>
<p class="tr">Non-attachment is the mastery of one who no longer thirsts for objects seen or heard of.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “That effort, which comes to those who have given up their thirst after objects either seen or heard, and which wills to control the objects, is non-attachment.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.15</p>
</div>

Patanjali names two sources of craving: what we've **seen** ourselves, and what we've **heard** about, from others, from advertisements, from promises of heaven. Both throw stones into the lake.

Vivekananda makes it vivid. Someone takes my watch in the street: that's my own experience, and instantly my mind rises in a wave of anger. "Allow that not to come," he says. If you can, you have vairāgya. Then the second source: the worldly-minded teach us that sense enjoyment is the highest goal. Not letting the mind take that shape either is renunciation. His summary:

<div class="aside">

“These should be controlled by me, and not I by them.”

</div>

Non-attachment isn't coldness. It's being free. You can still enjoy a meal, love your family, do your work with full heart. What changes is that your peace no longer depends on any of it.

### The highest non-attachment

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तत्परं पुरुषख्यातेर्गुणवैतृष्ण्यम्</p>
<p class="iast">tat paraṃ puruṣa-khyāter guṇa-vaitṛṣṇyam</p>
<p class="tr">The highest form of it, from knowing the Purusha, is freedom from thirst for even the qualities of nature.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “That extreme non-attachment, giving up even the qualities, shows (the real nature of) the Purusa.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.16</p>
</div>

Ordinary non-attachment lets go of particular things. The highest kind comes from seeing the self, the Purusha, directly, and then nothing in nature, not even its finest pleasures or powers, has any pull. In Vivekananda's reading, the self is beyond nature, shining by its own light; all the intelligence we see in nature is only its reflection.

## Putting them together

- **Each day, practise.** Sit, even for ten minutes. Return to the breath, the mantra or the witness every time you wander. That's abhyāsa.
- **Each day, let go of one thing.** Notice one craving, one grudge, one "I need this", and don't feed it. That's vairāgya.

One builds the new habit. The other stops feeding the old ones. Between them, the lake settles.
