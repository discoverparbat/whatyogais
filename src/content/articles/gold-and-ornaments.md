[Part 3](/the-texts/maya-rope-and-snake/) said that what we experience is Brahman covered with name and form. If that's true, it should be possible to point out, in any experience at all, which part is Brahman and which part is the world. Verse 20 does exactly that. Swami Sarvapriyananda calls it spiritual archaeology: digging God out of the world, right where we stand.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">अस्ति भाति प्रियं रूपं नाम चेत्यंशपञ्चकम् ।<br>आद्यत्रयं ब्रह्मरूपं जगद्रूपं ततो द्वयम् ॥</p>
<p class="iast">asti bhāti priyaṃ rūpaṃ nāma cety aṃśa-pañcakam<br>ādya-trayaṃ brahma-rūpaṃ jagad-rūpaṃ tato dvayam</p>
<p class="tr">It is, it shines, it is dear, form and name: these are the five aspects. The first three are the nature of Brahman; the other two are the nature of the world.</p>
<p class="ref">Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka 20</p>
</div>

## The five parts of any experience

Pick anything: a chair, a person, a song, a memory.

| Aspect | Meaning | Belongs to |
|---|---|---|
| **asti** | it *is*: it exists | Brahman as *sat*, existence |
| **bhāti** | it *shines*: it shows up in awareness | Brahman as *cit*, consciousness |
| **priyam** | it is *dear*: it gives some joy | Brahman as *ānanda*, bliss |
| **rūpa** | its form | the world (Maya) |
| **nāma** | its name | the world (Maya) |

The chair *is*. The person *is*. Even empty space *is*. We never notice, because it's everywhere. And everything we know *shines* in the light of awareness; nothing is experienced without it. What differs from thing to thing is only the last two: shape and label.

## Gold and ornaments

The oldest example in Vedanta is gold. An earring, a necklace and a bangle look different and have different names, but all of them are gold. Melt the earrings and make a necklace: the name and form change, the gold doesn't. Swami Sarvapriyananda tells two stories about it.

A wife tells her husband she's bored of her earrings and wants them made into a necklace; it won't cost a thing. He sighs: woman, it's the same gold whether you wear it on your ears or round your neck.

And a man in hard times takes a gold Ganesha, with Ganesha's little mouse at his feet, to a pawnbroker. The pawnbroker weighs it and names a price. "And the mouse?" asks the man. Same rate. The man is outraged: the same price for Lord Ganesha and a mouse? Have you no religion? The pawnbroker shrugs: to you it's Ganesha and a mouse. To me it's gold.

(When the swami told this to a hall full of goldsmiths, one came up afterwards and complained that he'd ruin their trade. If people learn it's all the same gold, who will pay to have it remade?)

The other classic pictures say the same: a table is wood, which is why we say "touch wood", not "touch table". A wave, foam, a tsunami: all water. The pot is clay. **The only thing there is the gold, the wood, the water. The rest is name and form.**

One caution the swami adds: these examples aren't about Brahman being *shaped* into a world, as a carpenter shapes wood. Only one point is meant: the entire reality of the table is the wood. The wood can exist without the table, as a tree or as sawdust; the table can't exist for a moment without the wood.

## Then why isn't everything joyful?

A fair objection: existence is everywhere, but bliss? Most of life isn't blissful. The traditional answer uses sunlight. Everything reflects sunlight, but only water or glass forms an image of the sun. In the same way:

- **existence** shows up in everything
- **consciousness** shows up only in minds, which is why you never feel the table's awareness, only your own
- **bliss** shows up only in a mind in a calm, clear (*sāttvika*) state, which is why happiness is always a particular state of mind

Brahman is everywhere in full. It's what can reflect it that varies.

## Everything, without exception

<p class="dev" lang="sa">खवाय्वग्निजलोर्वीषु देवतिर्यङ्नरादिषु ।<br>अभिन्नाः सच्चिदानन्दा भिद्यते रूपनामनी ॥</p>
<p class="iast">kha-vāyv-agni-jalorvīṣu deva-tiryaṅ-narādiṣu<br>abhinnāḥ sac-cid-ānandā bhidyate rūpa-nāmanī</p>
<p class="tr">In space, air, fire, water and earth, in gods, animals and humans, existence-consciousness-bliss is the same. Only name and form differ.</p>

## How to practise it

Here the teaching becomes something to do, with eyes open, anywhere. Swami Sarvapriyananda's suggestion: instead of seeing a microphone, a table and a scarf, see **existence with the name and form of a microphone**, existence with the name and form of a table. Imagine yourself surrounded by an ocean of existence, in which people and chairs and planets are waves.

This is why the sages in the Gita are called *sama-darśinaḥ*, those who see the same everywhere. Not because they can't tell a friend from an enemy or gold from clay, but because what they attend to in both is the same existence. Ramakrishna would throw a coin and a lump of earth into the Ganges together to make the point.

And it isn't far away. Swami Vivekananda called it the open secret. The swami tells of a thief on an overnight train who searches a sleeping businessman's things all night for his money and finds nothing. In the morning, defeated, he asks where it was. "Under your pillow," says the businessman. God has hidden himself in the last place we'd look: right here, in every ordinary thing.
