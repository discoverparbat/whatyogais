This practice turns one famous verse into ten minutes of sitting. The verse, from the *Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka*, moves step by step: the world is seen by the eyes, the eyes by the mind, the mind by the witness, and the witness is never seen. We explain it in full in [the seer and the seen](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/). Here you check it for yourself.

You don't need to believe anything for this. You only need to notice.

## Before you start

- **Sit comfortably**, on a cushion or a chair, back upright but not stiff. Patanjali's only rule for posture is that it should be steady and easy.
- **Set a timer** for ten minutes, so you aren't checking the clock.
- **Close your eyes**, or rest them half-open on the floor in front of you.
- Take three slow breaths, then let the breath go back to its own rhythm.

## The practice

### 1. Sounds (two minutes)

Listen to whatever sounds are around: traffic, birds, a fan, your own breathing. Don't name them or follow them. Notice that they come and go, near and far, loud and quiet.

Then notice one simple fact: **the sounds are heard, and you are hearing them.** They change; the hearing doesn't go anywhere.

### 2. The body (two minutes)

Now turn to the body. Feel its weight on the seat. The touch of air on your skin. Warmth, tightness, an itch, the rise and fall of the breath.

These sensations are also known. Some are pleasant, some are not, and they keep shifting. **The body is felt, and you are the one feeling it.** Something knows "my knee aches" or "my hands are warm". That knower isn't the knee or the hands.

### 3. Thoughts and feelings (three minutes)

Now notice the mind itself. Thoughts will come: a plan, a memory, a worry, a comment on how the practice is going. Feelings will come: restlessness, calm, boredom.

Don't push them away and don't follow them. Treat each one like a passing car seen from a window: there it comes, there it goes. If you get carried off, that's fine. The moment you notice you were lost in thought, you've already stepped back. Simply begin again.

**Thoughts are seen, and you are the one aware of them.** Many thoughts, one awareness. The thoughts keep changing. The awareness doesn't.

### 4. Rest as the one who knows (three minutes)

Now, instead of looking at any sound, sensation or thought, rest your attention on **the noticing itself**.

You'll find you can't see it like an object, and that's the point the verse makes. Don't go looking for it. Just rest in the plain fact that you are aware, now. Sounds, sensations and thoughts can come and go inside that awareness. Let them. You don't have to do anything about them.

If it helps, ask quietly once: *what is aware right now?* Then don't answer with a thought. Just stay with the question.

When the timer goes, open your eyes slowly and sit for a few more breaths before you get up.

## Taking it off the cushion

The real test is ordinary life. A few times a day, when something stirs you up (an annoying message, a rush of worry, a moment of pleasure), pause and notice: *this is known.* There's the feeling, and there's the one aware of the feeling.

This won't stop the feeling. Pain still hurts and worries still come. But it puts a little room between you and it, and in that room it often loosens its grip. The texts say that room was always there. The practice is just noticing it.

<div class="aside">

**A note of care:** if you're going through something hard, such as grief, trauma or a period of deep anxiety, gentle practices like this can bring strong feelings to the surface. That's normal, but you don't have to go through it alone. Keep sessions short, keep your eyes open if that feels safer, and talk to a teacher or a professional you trust.

</div>

## Why this is yoga

Patanjali defines yoga as the stilling of the mind's movements, and says that then the seer rests in its own nature (*Yoga Sutras* 1.2–1.3). The rest of the time, he says, the seer seems to take on the shape of those movements (1.4): we feel we *are* the worry, the mood, the plan. This practice is a small, daily way of undoing that confusion, one noticing at a time.
