    a: "Prana is the life force: the energy that animates body and mind, working in five currents (prana, apana, samana, udana, vyana) that govern breathing, elimination, digestion, upward movement and circulation.   - q: "What is the difference between dharana, dhyana and samadhi?"
    a: "Dharana is holding the mind on one object of meditation while the senses stay withdrawn. Dhyana is that same holding, unbroken and sustained for longer.   - q: "What are the eight limbs of yoga?"
    a: "Yama (ethical restraints), niyama (personal disciplines), asana (posture), pranayama (control of the flow of prana, loosely breath control), pratyahara (withdrawal of the senses), dharana (holding the mind on one object), dhyana (meditation, that holding made unbroken and longer) and samadhi (absorption, the state of realisation).   - q: "Do the eight limbs have to be practised in order?"
    a: "They're listed in a logical order, from outer to inner, and each supports the next. In practice most people work on several at once: you don't have to perfect ethics before you learn to breathe well. The inner limbs, though, really do depend on a steady body and a settled breath."

Yoga didn't begin with Patanjali. The *Bhagavad Gita* had already taught four paths of yoga: knowledge (*jñāna*), action (*karma*), meditation (*dhyāna*) and devotion (*bhakti*). We explain all four in [the four paths of yoga](/what-yoga-is/four-paths-of-yoga/). What Patanjali did was take one of them, the path of meditation, and set it out as a precise method.

In the second chapter of the *Yoga Sutras*, he gives that method: a practical path to the still mind he defined at the start. It has eight parts, which is why it's called *aṣṭāṅga*, "eight-limbed".

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">यमनियमासनप्राणायामप्रत्याहारधारणाध्यानसमाधयोऽष्टावङ्गानि</p>
<p class="iast">yama-niyama-āsana-prāṇāyāma-pratyāhāra-dhāraṇā-dhyāna-samādhayo ’ṣṭāv aṅgāni</p>
<p class="tr">Restraint, discipline, posture, breath regulation, withdrawal of the senses, concentration, meditation and absorption are the eight limbs.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama, Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi, are the limbs of Yoga.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.29</p>
</div>

The order matters. It moves from how you treat the world, to your own habits, to the body, to the breath, and then inward, into the mind. The first five are sometimes called the outer limbs; the last three, the inner.

## 1. Yama: how you treat others

The five *yamas* are restraints, things you hold back from:

- **Ahiṃsā:** non-harm, in action, word and thought
- **Satya:** truthfulness
- **Asteya:** not stealing, including time, credit and attention
- **Brahmacarya:** moderation, traditionally celibacy, more widely the wise use of energy
- **Aparigraha:** non-possessiveness, not grasping

Patanjali calls these the "great vow" when practised everywhere, at all times. They come first because a mind full of conflict, lies and craving can't become still, however good your posture.

## 2. Niyama: how you treat yourself

The five *niyamas* are disciplines you take up:

- **Śauca:** cleanliness, of body and mind
- **Santoṣa:** contentment
- **Tapas:** discipline, the willingness to do what is hard
- **Svādhyāya:** self-study, including study of the texts
- **Īśvara-praṇidhāna:** surrender to the divine, or to something larger than yourself

## 3. Āsana: a steady, comfortable seat

Here is the limb most people know, and Patanjali's whole teaching on it fits in three sutras. Posture should be *sthira* and *sukha*, steady and easy. Its purpose is to let you sit, without strain, long enough for the inner work. There are no named poses in the Yoga Sutras at all. More in [what the Yoga Sutras actually say about asana](/the-texts/yoga-sutras-on-asana/).

## 4. Prāṇāyāma: control of the flow of prāṇa

*Prāṇāyāma* is usually translated as "breath control", but that's a loose translation. It means the control (*āyāma*, also "extension") of the flow of **prāṇa**.

### What is prāṇa?

Prāṇa is the life force: the energy that animates the body and mind. It's what breathes in you, digests your food, moves your blood and keeps you alive; when it leaves, the body is a corpse, however healthy its parts. The yoga texts describe it working in five currents (*vāyus*): **prāṇa** (inward, in the chest, the breath taken in), **apāna** (downward and outward: elimination), **samāna** (in the belly: digestion and assimilation), **udāna** (upward: speech, and the soul's exit at death) and **vyāna** (pervading the whole body: circulation).

Breath isn't prāṇa itself. It's the most visible movement of prāṇa, and the one handle on it we can easily grasp. Swami Vivekananda compared the breath to the flywheel of a great machine: take hold of the flywheel, and you begin to govern the whole engine. That's why the practice starts with the breath, and why it's called breath control.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तस्मिन्सति श्वासप्रश्वासयोर्गतिविच्छेदः प्राणायामः</p>
<p class="iast">tasmin sati śvāsa-praśvāsayor gati-vicchedaḥ prāṇāyāmaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Once posture is steady, prāṇāyāma is the regulation of the flow of inhalation and exhalation.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Controlling the motion of the exhalation and the inhalation follows after this.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.49</p>
</div>

Prāṇa and mind move together: when the breath is restless the mind is restless, and when the breath is still the mind becomes still. Patanjali says pranayama thins the veil over the mind's inner light and makes it fit for concentration (2.52–2.53). Traditional teachers treat it with care and teach it gradually. More in [pranayama: what breathwork really is](/beyond-the-mat/pranayama/).

## 5. Pratyāhāra: withdrawal of the senses

The senses usually run outward, after every sound, sight, smell and notification, and drag the mind with them. *Pratyāhāra* is drawing them back in: the senses stop chasing their objects and follow the mind inward instead, the way a tortoise draws in its limbs (Bhagavad Gita 2.58).

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">स्वविषयासंप्रयोगे चित्तस्वरूपानुकार इवेन्द्रियाणां प्रत्याहारः</p>
<p class="iast">sva-viṣayāsaṃprayoge citta-svarūpānukāra ivendriyāṇāṃ pratyāhāraḥ</p>
<p class="tr">When the senses are disengaged from their objects and, as it were, follow the nature of the mind, that is pratyāhāra.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “The drawing in of the organs is by their giving up their own objects and taking the form of the mind-stuff.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.54</p>
</div>

Sounds are still there; you just stop going out to them. Pratyāhāra is the hinge of the eight limbs: the first five turn us from the world toward ourselves, and the last three work entirely within.

## 6. Dhāraṇā: holding the mind on one object

With the senses withdrawn, and *while keeping them withdrawn*, the mind is now fixed on one object of meditation: the breath, a mantra, a point such as the heart or the space between the eyebrows, the form of a deity, a light.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">देशबन्धश्चित्तस्य धारणा</p>
<p class="iast">deśa-bandhaś cittasya dhāraṇā</p>
<p class="tr">Dhāraṇā is binding the mind to one place.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Dharana is holding the mind on to some particular object.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 3.1</p>
</div>

The mind wanders, and you bring it back. And again. That bringing back, gently and without frustration, *is* dhāraṇā.

## 7. Dhyāna: dhāraṇā, unbroken and longer

When that holding stops being interrupted, when the mind stays on its object in an unbroken flow for a long time, without having to be dragged back, dhāraṇā has become *dhyāna*, meditation.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तत्र प्रत्ययैकतानता ध्यानम्</p>
<p class="iast">tatra pratyayaika-tānatā dhyānam</p>
<p class="tr">An unbroken flow of awareness toward that object is dhyāna.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “An unbroken flow of knowledge to that object is Dhyana.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 3.2</p>
</div>

Vivekananda puts the difference simply: when the mind succeeds in holding to its object, that is dhāraṇā; when it keeps itself in that state for some time, it is dhyāna. Patanjali's word is *ekatānatā*: a single, continuous stretch, like oil poured from one vessel into another without breaking into drops, as the commentators say.

## 8. Samādhi: the state of realisation

Finally, even the sense of "me meditating on this" falls away. Only the object shines; the meditator, as a separate self, is forgotten.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तदेवार्थमात्रनिर्भासं स्वरूपशून्यमिव समाधिः</p>
<p class="iast">tad evārtha-mātra-nirbhāsaṃ svarūpa-śūnyam iva samādhiḥ</p>
<p class="tr">When that same meditation shines with the object alone, as if empty of its own form, it is samādhi.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “When that, giving up all forms, reflects only the meaning, it is Samadhi.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 3.3</p>
</div>

Patanjali describes samādhi in stages. In the first, still "with seed" (*sabīja*), the mind is absorbed in an object, gross or subtle. In the highest, "without seed" (*nirbīja*), even that last support dissolves. Then, in the words of the third sutra, the seer rests in its own nature (1.3). This is the **state of realisation**: the goal the whole eight-limbed path exists for, and what Vedanta calls knowing the Self as Brahman. See [Patanjali's definition of yoga](/the-texts/patanjali-definition-of-yoga/) and [the six meditations of Vedanta](/beyond-the-mat/six-vedantic-meditations/).

<div class="aside">

**Dhāraṇā, dhyāna, samādhi: one deepening.** Patanjali groups the last three together as **saṃyama** (3.4). They aren't three separate techniques. They're one movement of attention: holding the mind on an object (dhāraṇā), holding it there unbroken for long (dhyāna), until only the object shines and the meditator disappears (samādhi). Pratyāhāra keeps the doors closed throughout.

</div>

## What this means for your practice

If you go to a yoga class, you're already practising part of limb three, and probably some of four in the breathing. The rest of the path is still there, waiting: a little honesty and kindness off the mat, a few minutes of breath practice, ten minutes of sitting still. That's how the tradition always intended the poses to be used.
