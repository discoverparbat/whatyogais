faqs:
  - q: "What is turiya?"
    a: "Turiya (turīya) simply means 'the fourth'. In the Mandukya Upanishad it is the self beyond the three states of waking, dreaming and deep sleep: pure awareness. It is not a fourth state you enter later. It is the awareness that is already present in all three, the way gold is present in a bangle, a necklace and a ring."
  - q: "What are the four states of consciousness in the Mandukya Upanishad?"
    a: "Waking (jāgrat), whose experiencer is called Vishva and whose awareness faces outward; dreaming (svapna), whose experiencer is Taijasa and whose awareness faces inward, in the mind; deep sleep (suṣupti), whose experiencer is Prajna, an undivided mass of awareness with nothing to illumine; and the fourth (turīya), the self that underlies and illumines all three. The Upanishad says the first three come and go and only the fourth is real."
  - q: "Why is the Mandukya Upanishad so important?"
    a: "It is the shortest of the major Upanishads, only 12 verses in the Atharva Veda, and is often called the hardest. A traditional saying in the Muktika Upanishad holds that for someone seeking liberation, the Mandukya alone is enough. Gaudapada, the teacher of Shankara's teacher, wrote verses on it (the Mandukya Karika), and Shankara commented on both."
  - q: "What is the difference between deep sleep and turiya?"
    a: "In both there is no sense of a separate world or a separate self. But deep sleep is covered by ignorance: you know nothing, not even yourself. Turiya is the same oneness with the light on: awareness knowing itself, with no ignorance and no error. Gaudapada puts it as sleep without dream (deep sleep) versus neither sleep nor dream (turiya)."
  - q: "Do I need to stop my thoughts to realise turiya?"
    a: "According to Advaita Vedanta, no. Deep sleep is free of thought every night and it has not made anyone realised. The method Vedanta teaches is inquiry (vicāra) in the waking state: looking back over waking, dream and sleep and noticing what was present through all three. Meditation and samadhi can deepen and steady that understanding."

Who am I? Not my name or my job: those can be lost overnight, and people who lose them often find they no longer know who they are. The Upanishads ask the question at its deepest level, and the shortest of them answers it most directly. The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* has only twelve verses, sits in the Atharva Veda, and has a reputation as the hardest of them all. A traditional saying goes that for anyone who truly wants liberation, the Mandukya alone is enough.

Its whole teaching rests on something you did last night and will do again tonight: you woke, you dreamed and you slept.

## A king's nightmare

Swami Sarvapriyananda opens his talks on this Upanishad with a story he heard from a monk in Gangotri.

King Janaka is woken by a guard: the enemy has attacked. He leads his army out, is defeated, wounded and dragged before the conquering king, who spares his life but banishes him. In his own capital no one dares give him food or water. After two days on foot he crosses the border and joins a line of beggars waiting for rice, only for the food to run out just as he reaches the front. They scrape the burnt starch from the bottom of the pot for him. As he lifts the bowl to his lips, a kite swoops down and knocks it into the dust. Janaka collapses, weeping.

Then a voice: "Maharaj, what happened?" He wakes on his own bed, drenched in sweat, the guard at the door. It was a dream.

From that moment the king will say only one thing, to his queen, his ministers, everyone: *Is this true, or was that true?* Nothing gets done. Finally the sage Ashtavakra arrives and asks him: when you lay in the dust, beaten and starving, was your palace there, your queen, your army? No. And now, sitting on your throne, is the defeat there, the wounds, the hunger? No. Then neither is true. But were *you* there in the dream? Yes, I saw it all. Are you here now? Yes. Then *you*, the one who witnessed both, you alone are true.

The story and the Upanishad say the same thing. The rest of this article is the working-out.

## Three states, one of you

The Upanishad begins by saying the self has four "quarters" or aspects:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">सर्वं ह्येतद् ब्रह्मायमात्मा ब्रह्म सोऽयमात्मा चतुष्पात् ॥</p>
<p class="iast">sarvaṃ hy etad brahmāyam ātmā brahma so 'yam ātmā catuṣpāt</p>
<p class="tr">All this is Brahman. This self is Brahman. This self has four aspects.</p>
<p class="ref">Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad 2</p>
</div>

The first three are the states we all pass through every day. The Upanishad gives each a name, and they're worth learning:

| State | The experiencer | What is experienced | Awareness is… |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waking (*jāgrat*) | **Vishva**, "you" right now | the waking world, through body and senses | turned outward (*bahiṣprajña*) |
| Dreaming (*svapna*) | **Taijasa**, the dreamer | a dream world made entirely by the mind | turned inward (*antaḥprajña*) |
| Deep sleep (*suṣupti*) | **Prajna**, the sleeper | blankness: no world, no body, no thought | a single undivided mass (*prajñānaghana*) |
| The fourth (*turīya*) | the self itself | — | the awareness illumining all three |

A few things to notice. The dream world is built from the material of waking life, which is why Vedanta calls it "inward": nothing is happening to the body under the blanket, yet you travel, talk and run. In deep sleep, "no body and no mind" is meant from your own side. Someone else sees you snoring under the mosquito net; you experience nothing at all, not even the thought "I am asleep". And deep sleep isn't nothing: everything is folded up in it, ready, because the moment you wake, the whole world and all your responsibilities are back.

The Upanishad's claim is startling: **you are not the waker, not the dreamer and not the sleeper. You are the fourth.** *Turīya* only means "fourth", and it isn't even the Upanishad's own word; the verse says *caturtha*, fourth, and the name turiya comes later, from Gaudapada.

## Not a fourth thing: gold in three ornaments

If you hear "the fourth", you'll picture a fourth state, rarer and higher, waiting somewhere beyond sleep. That's exactly the mistake the teaching warns against.

Think of a bangle, a necklace and a ring. Someone tells you they're all just gold. If you don't understand what gold is, you might throw the jewellery away and go looking for "gold" somewhere else. You won't find it: there is no gold apart from these. Or you might decide the bangle *is* the gold, so that when it's melted down and made into a necklace, you think the gold is gone. Both are wrong. Gold is never found apart from the ornaments, yet it outlasts every one of them. (We tell the full gold story, and the pawnbroker who weighed Lord Ganesha and his mouse at the same rate, in [Gold and ornaments](/the-texts/gold-and-ornaments/).)

So it is with the three states. The waker isn't the dreamer; whatever happens in the dream is falsified when you wake. The dreamer isn't the sleeper. Each state excludes the others, like bangle and necklace. And yet you say *my* waking, *my* dream, *my* deep sleep. Who is that "I" common to all three? That is turiya: apart from each state, present in and through all of them. In another of the swami's images it's a shopkeeper who trades all day, relaxes in front of the television in the evening and then goes to sleep. Three rooms, three activities, three names, one man.

The same is true of everything that changes. Your body at ten, twenty and now; your mind at your school exams and your mind today, with entirely different knowledge, likes and goals. What has stayed the same, so that you can say "I was that child"? Only the awareness watching the changes. The swami's station master makes the same point; we tell that story in [The mirror of the mind](/the-texts/mirror-of-the-mind/).

## The seventh verse, line by line

The heart of the Upanishad is its seventh verse, a single sentence. Swami Sarvapriyananda says he has found nothing more profound in any philosophy he has read.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">नान्तःप्रज्ञं न बहिष्प्रज्ञं नोभयतःप्रज्ञं न प्रज्ञानघनं न प्रज्ञं नाप्रज्ञम् ।<br>अदृष्टमव्यवहार्यमग्राह्यमलक्षणमचिन्त्यमव्यपदेश्यम्<br>एकात्मप्रत्ययसारं प्रपञ्चोपशमं शान्तं शिवमद्वैतं चतुर्थं मन्यन्ते स आत्मा स विज्ञेयः ॥</p>
<p class="iast">nāntaḥ-prajñaṃ na bahiṣ-prajñaṃ nobhayataḥ-prajñaṃ na prajñāna-ghanaṃ na prajñaṃ nāprajñam<br>adṛṣṭam avyavahāryam agrāhyam alakṣaṇam acintyam avyapadeśyam<br>ekātma-pratyaya-sāraṃ prapañcopaśamaṃ śāntaṃ śivam advaitaṃ caturthaṃ manyante sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Not turned inward, not turned outward, not turned both ways, not a mass of awareness, not knowing, not unknowing. Unseen, beyond dealings, ungraspable, without marks, unthinkable, unnameable. Its essence is the sense of being one self; the world is stilled in it; peaceful, auspicious, non-dual. This is considered the fourth. That is the self. That is to be known.</p>
<p class="ref">Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad 7</p>
</div>

It moves in three steps.

**1. You are not the three experiencers.** Not turned inward: not the dreamer. Not turned outward: not the waker. Not "both ways": not even the hazy in-between of drifting off in a long lecture. Not a mass of awareness: not the deep sleeper. Not "knowing" (Shankara reads this as: not an all-knowing God either) and not "unknowing": not inert like a stone.

**2. You are not anything in the three worlds.** It can't be seen, heard or touched; it's not an object for the senses. That doesn't make it unreal, any more than you can't see a Higgs boson. It can't be used or dealt with (*avyavahārya*), yet nothing can be done without it. It can't be grasped, or reached on pilgrimage. It can't be inferred, the way you infer fire from smoke, because there's no sign that points to it. It can't be thought, though every thought is lit by it.

And it can't be named. Shankara explains why: words work in only five ways. By class (that animal is a cow), by quality (bring the red flower), by action (call the cook), by relation (the teacher of that student), or by convention (his parents named him Prakash). Turiya belongs to no class, has no qualities, does nothing, has nothing second to it to be related to, and can't be pointed out to be given a name.

**3. Then, what it is.** *Ekātma-pratyaya-sāra*: its essence is the plain, constant sense of "I", the "I" that was there in the dream and is here now, exactly what Ashtavakra pointed Janaka to. Follow that sense of "I" inward, the way Ramana Maharshi taught people to ask "Who am I?", and you arrive here. *Prapañcopaśama*: the three worlds subside in it; their troubles never touch it. *Śāntam*: peace. *Śivam*: auspicious, joy. *Advaitam*: non-dual, because nothing exists apart from it, as no ornament exists apart from gold.

That last word has a remarkable consequence. When you wake from a dream, you say the whole dream, every person and place in it, was made of your own mind: you were all of it. The Upanishad says the same of the waking world. If nothing exists apart from turiya, then the waker and the entire waking world are turiya too. This is what lets a sage say, "I am one with everything."

Finally the verse says the fourth "is considered" (*manyante*) as fourth: people *think* of it as a fourth thing, but really it's the only thing. The three are appearances; there aren't four realities here, only one. *Sa ātmā*: that is the self, the answer to "who am I". *Sa vijñeyaḥ*: that is to be known, not just heard about in a lecture, but realised as a living fact.

## Sleep, dream and waking up: Gaudapada's verses

Gaudapada, the teacher of Shankara's teacher, wrote verses (*kārikā*) on the Mandukya. In the first chapter he sharpens the difference between the four with two ideas: **ignorance** (*agrahaṇa*, not grasping what you are) and **error** (*anyathā-grahaṇa*, grasping it wrongly). It's the rope and the snake again: not seeing the rope is the cause; seeing a snake is the effect. (More on that picture in [Maya: the rope and the snake](/the-texts/maya-rope-and-snake/).)

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">कार्यकारणबद्धौ ताविष्येते विश्वतैजसौ ।<br>प्राज्ञः कारणबद्धस्तु द्वौ तौ तुर्ये न सिध्यतः ॥</p>
<p class="iast">kārya-kāraṇa-baddhau tāv iṣyete viśva-taijasau<br>prājñaḥ kāraṇa-baddhas tu dvau tau turye na sidhyataḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Vishva and Taijasa are bound by both effect and cause. Prajna is bound by the cause alone. In Turiya, neither exists.</p>
<p class="ref">Māṇḍūkya Kārikā 1.11</p>
</div>

| | Ignorance (cause) | Error (effect) |
|---|---|---|
| Waker (Vishva) | yes: doesn't know itself as awareness | yes: "I am this body; the world is separate from me" |
| Dreamer (Taijasa) | yes | yes: a whole separate dream world and dream body |
| Deep sleeper (Prajna) | yes: knows nothing, not even itself | **no:** no separate world, no suffering |
| The fourth (Turiya) | **no** | **no:** the world may appear, but as oneself |

Deep sleep is interesting. A rope in total darkness can't be mistaken for a snake; you only get the error in half-light. So in deep sleep there's no error, no division, no suffering: even a patient in great pain is free of it while deeply asleep. And everyone's deep sleep is the same, a scholar's and a mosquito's, because it's pure darkness with nothing to tell apart.

Gaudapada then calls ignorance **sleep** (*nidrā*) and error **dream** (*svapna*), which leads somewhere unexpected:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">स्वप्ननिद्रायुतावाद्यौ प्राज्ञस्त्वस्वप्ननिद्रया ।<br>न निद्रां नैव च स्वप्नं तुर्ये पश्यन्ति निश्चिताः ॥</p>
<p class="iast">svapna-nidrā-yutāv ādyau prājñas tv asvapna-nidrayā<br>na nidrāṃ naiva ca svapnaṃ turye paśyanti niścitāḥ</p>
<p class="tr">The first two are joined with dream and sleep. Prajna has sleep without dream. Those who know see neither sleep nor dream in Turiya.</p>
<p class="ref">Māṇḍūkya Kārikā 1.14</p>
</div>

By this definition, your waking life is a dream too: you are asleep to what you are, and dreaming that you're a limited person with friends, enemies, goals and failures. This is where the Vedantic saying that life is like a dream comes from. It's also why, when a shepherd boy asked the Buddha whether he was a god, an angel or a man, he answered no to each and said: *I am awake.*

And deep sleep versus turiya? In neither is there a second thing. But Prajna is covered in darkness, while turiya is the same oneness with the lights on. The swami's image: a car's headlights at the crest of a hill on a clear, moonless night. With no insects or rain in the beam, you can't see the light at all, yet it's blazing. Deep sleep is awareness with nothing to reflect it, which is why the neuroscientist says there's no consciousness in deep sleep and the Vedantin says there's *only* consciousness there.

The snake can't be chased away with a stick. It goes only when the rope is seen. In the same way, ignorance is removed only by knowledge, and the error goes with it. And when did the ignorance begin? Gaudapada answers that question in advance:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">अनादिमायया सुप्तो यदा जीवः प्रबुध्यते ।<br>अजमनिद्रमस्वप्नमद्वैतं बुध्यते तदा ॥</p>
<p class="iast">anādi-māyayā supto yadā jīvaḥ prabudhyate<br>ajam anidram asvapnam advaitaṃ budhyate tadā</p>
<p class="tr">When the individual, asleep in beginningless Maya, wakes up, then it realises the unborn, sleepless, dreamless, non-dual.</p>
<p class="ref">Māṇḍūkya Kārikā 1.16</p>
</div>

## How do you realise it?

Not by going into deep sleep. Thoughtlessness happens to all of us every night, and it hasn't made anyone realised. Nor, according to Advaita Vedanta, do you have to shut the mind down first.

The swami's example is a village boy at his first film. On the way, his father explains: there's a screen, and pictures are projected on it. They arrive late, the film is running, and after a while the boy asks: *where's the screen?* Wherever the father points, there's a picture: Krishna, Arjuna, a chariot. The boy only gets it at the interval, when the lights come up on a blank white screen. That's like samadhi: stop the pictures and see the screen. But afterwards, when the film starts again, he never asks the question again. The screen is covered in pictures and he knows exactly where it is.

That's the Vedantic method. Here, in the waking state, by *vicāra*, inquiry, look back over waking, dreaming and sleep and notice what was there through all of them. You don't have to fall asleep to examine sleep; you reflect on it now. Ramakrishna said that without direct experience the conviction never becomes firm, so meditation and samadhi strengthen it. But the recognition itself is possible right here, pictures and all. Our [witness meditation](/beyond-the-mat/witness-meditation/) is a simple place to start.

## Why it matters

Swami Vivekananda said that the one who runs away from the world to die in a Himalayan cave has missed the way, and so has the one who plunges into the world's pleasures. The way is to spiritualise everyday life. The Mandukya shows what that means. Searching for turiya somewhere else, away from your waking, dreaming and sleeping, is like throwing away the jewellery to look for gold. Clinging to the waker as all you are is trouble too: that waker will grow old, fall ill and die.

The same awareness appears as you, as everyone you meet and as the world itself. Seen that way, you don't leave the world to find what you are. You recognise it in the middle of the world, the way a sage sees the same in friend and enemy, in a coin and in a clod of earth. That, in the end, is what [yoga is for](/what-yoga-is/what-is-yoga/): using this human life, waking, dreaming and sleeping, to realise who you really are.
