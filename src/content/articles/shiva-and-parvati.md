Most love stories end at the wedding. This one uses a marriage to describe the deepest relationship there is: between consciousness and everything it gives life to.

## The story

### Sati

Shiva's first wife was **Satī**, daughter of the powerful king Dakṣa. Daksha despised his ash-smeared, wandering son-in-law, and when he held a great sacrifice he invited every god but Shiva. Sati went anyway. When her father insulted Shiva before the assembled gods, she could not bear it, and gave up her body in the sacrificial fire.

Shiva's grief was terrible. He took up her body and wandered the worlds. To end his grief, Vishnu cut the body piece by piece, and where each part fell, a seat of the goddess arose: the *Śakti Pīṭhas*. One of the most revered is **Guhyeśvarī**, beside the Bagmati, close to Pashupatinath in Kathmandu.

Shiva withdrew into meditation in the Himalaya, closed to the world.

### Parvati

Sati was reborn as **Pārvatī**, "daughter of the mountain", child of Himavat, the Himalaya himself. From childhood she knew she belonged to Shiva. But Shiva sat in deep meditation and would not open his eyes.

Meanwhile the world needed him: a demon could be defeated only by a son of Shiva. So the gods sent **Kāma**, the god of desire, to shoot his flower-arrows at the meditating god. Shiva opened his third eye, and Kama was burned to ash.

Parvati understood. Shiva could not be won by beauty or by desire. So she went into the mountains herself and undertook austerities harsher than any sage's: fasting, standing in the snow, living at last on nothing but air. Her devotion finally moved Shiva. He came to her disguised as a young ascetic, mocking the Shiva she loved to test her, and when she defended him without wavering, he revealed himself. They married in the Himalaya, and their son Kārttikeya defeated the demon.

Later art shows them as **Ardhanārīśvara**, the Lord who is half woman: one figure, the right half Shiva, the left half Parvati.

## What the tradition reads in it

### Consciousness and power

In the tantric and yogic traditions, the pair describe the two aspects of reality. **Shiva** is pure consciousness: still, unchanging, the witness. **Shakti**, here Parvati, is power, energy, the dynamic force through which consciousness acts and appears as a world. The famous opening of the *Saundarya Laharī* says it:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">शिवः शक्त्या युक्तो यदि भवति शक्तः प्रभवितुं<br>न चेदेवं देवो न खलु कुशलः स्पन्दितुमपि</p>
<p class="iast">śivaḥ śaktyā yukto yadi bhavati śaktaḥ prabhavituṃ<br>na ced evaṃ devo na khalu kuśalaḥ spanditum api</p>
<p class="tr">Only when united with Shakti is Shiva able to create. Otherwise, the god cannot even stir.</p>
<p class="ref">Saundarya Laharī 1</p>
</div>

Shiva without Shakti is like a corpse, the tantric texts say; Shakti without Shiva has nothing to be the power *of*. That's why Ardhanarishvara is one body.

Different schools read the pair differently. In Patanjali's Sāṅkhya-based yoga, the seer (*puruṣa*) and nature (*prakṛti*) are two separate realities, and freedom means telling them apart. In the tantric reading, Shiva and Shakti are one reality in two aspects. Advaita Vedanta speaks of Brahman and its power, Maya. The language differs; the picture of a still consciousness and a moving power is shared. See [Maya: the rope and the snake](/the-texts/maya-rope-and-snake/).

### Kundalini: Shakti rising to Shiva

In the body, the tantric yoga texts say, Shakti lies coiled at the base of the spine as **kuṇḍalinī**, in the Mūlādhāra chakra. Shiva dwells at the crown, in the thousand-petalled Sahasrāra. The awakening of kundalini and her rise through the chakras is Shakti returning home to Shiva: Parvati's long journey to her husband, retold inside every practitioner. See [the chakras in the texts](/the-texts/chakras-texts-vs-rainbow/).

### Desire burned, love won

Kama, desire, is burned the moment he tries to force the meditating Shiva. Parvati wins him only through *tapas*, disciplined effort, and unwavering devotion. Teachers draw a plain lesson from it: the highest isn't reached by wanting it harder, but by becoming fit for it.

## The Nepal connection

For Nepal this is home ground. Parvati is the daughter of the Himalaya. Shiva is Pashupati, lord of Kathmandu's holiest temple (see [Pashupatinath and Shiva, the first yogi](/himalayan-roots/pashupatinath-shiva-first-yogi/)), and Guhyeshwari, the seat of Sati, stands just across the river. And every year at **Teej**, women across Nepal fast and pray, many without even water, in memory of Parvati's austerities.

## If you only remember one thing

Shiva is the still awareness; Parvati is the power that moves. Yoga, in the tantric reading, is their reunion, within you.
