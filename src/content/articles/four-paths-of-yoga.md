In most of the world, "yoga" means one thing: a posture class. Ask a little further and you'll usually hear about Patanjali and his eight limbs. But the tradition is wider and older than either. Long before the *Yoga Sutras*, the **Bhagavad Gita** taught yoga as several paths to the same freedom, each suited to a different kind of person.

## Yoga before Patanjali

The word *yoga* appears in the Upanishads centuries before Patanjali. The *Katha Upanishad* already defines it: "this steady holding of the senses is what they call yoga" (2.3.11). The *Bhagavad Gita*, which most scholars date earlier than the *Yoga Sutras*, uses the word on almost every page, and in many editions each of its eighteen chapters is titled as a yoga of its own.

Patanjali himself didn't claim to invent anything. His first sutra is *atha yogānuśāsanam*, "now, the teaching of yoga", and *anuśāsana* means instruction handed down. What he did was take one of the paths, **dhyāna yoga**, the path of meditation, and set it out as a precise, step-by-step method in eight limbs.

So the right picture is this: **four paths, and Patanjali's eight limbs as the detailed map of one of them.**

Swami Vivekananda, who made the four paths known worldwide, put the idea on the opening page of his book *Raja Yoga*:

<blockquote>
<p>"Each soul is potentially divine. The goal is to manifest this Divinity within, by controlling nature, external and internal. Do this either by work, or worship, or psychic control, or philosophy — by one, or more, or all of these — and be free."</p>
</blockquote>

Philosophy, work, psychic control and worship: jñāna, karma, dhyāna (which Vivekananda called *rāja yoga*) and bhakti. Different temperaments, one goal.

## 1. Jñāna yoga: the path of knowledge

Jñāna yoga is inquiry. It asks the most direct question of all: what am I? Its method is discernment (*viveka*): telling apart what you are from what you merely experience. The Gita's thirteenth chapter puts it in its own terms: the body is the field (*kṣetra*), and what knows it is the knower of the field (*kṣetrajña*). Knowing the difference is knowledge.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">न हि ज्ञानेन सदृशं पवित्रमिह विद्यते</p>
<p class="iast">na hi jñānena sadṛśaṃ pavitram iha vidyate</p>
<p class="tr">There is nothing in this world as purifying as knowledge.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 4.38</p>
</div>

Its texts are the Upanishads and the works of Vedanta. We follow one famous example step by step in [the seer and the seen](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/).

**Suits:** questioning, reflective people who need things to make sense before they can commit to them.

## 2. Karma yoga: the path of action

Karma yoga is doing your work fully and well, without being bound by what you get out of it. It's the Gita's first and most famous teaching:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन</p>
<p class="iast">karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana</p>
<p class="tr">Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 2.47</p>
</div>

A verse later the Gita defines yoga itself in these terms: *samatvaṃ yoga ucyate*, "evenness of mind is called yoga" (2.48), and then *yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam*, "yoga is skill in action" (2.50).

This doesn't mean not caring about results. It means acting because the work is right, not because of the reward, and staying even when it succeeds or fails. Done this way, ordinary life becomes practice: your job, your family, service to others. Karma yoga changes how you act in the world, and through that it cleans out the selfishness in the mind.

**Suits:** active people, people with responsibilities, anyone who finds sitting still hard.

## 3. Dhyāna yoga: the path of meditation

Dhyāna yoga works directly on the mind. The Gita's sixth chapter, often titled *Dhyāna Yoga*, teaches it plainly: sit alone in a clean, quiet place, on a seat neither too high nor too low; hold body, head and neck straight and still; draw the senses in and fix the mind within (6.10–6.13). Then:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">यथा दीपो निवातस्थो नेङ्गते सोपमा स्मृता</p>
<p class="iast">yathā dīpo nivātastho neṅgate sopamā smṛtā</p>
<p class="tr">As a lamp in a windless place does not flicker: that is the image of the yogi's controlled mind.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 6.19</p>
</div>

### Patanjali's eight limbs: the map of this path

This is the path Patanjali systematised. His *Yoga Sutras* define yoga as the stilling of the movements of the mind (1.2), and lay out eight limbs to get there: ethical restraint (*yama*), personal discipline (*niyama*), posture (*āsana*), breath (*prāṇāyāma*), withdrawal of the senses (*pratyāhāra*), concentration (*dhāraṇā*), meditation (*dhyāna*) and absorption (*samādhi*). Swami Vivekananda called this path **rāja yoga**, the royal yoga. We go through it in [Patanjali's definition of yoga](/the-texts/patanjali-definition-of-yoga/) and [the eight limbs, explained simply](/what-yoga-is/eight-limbs-of-yoga/).

**Hatha yoga**, the yoga of posture and breath that modern classes come from, belongs here too. The *Hatha Yoga Pradipika* says so at the start: it teaches hatha yoga for the sake of raja yoga. The body is prepared so the mind can be stilled. So a posture class, in the tradition's own terms, is a preparation within one limb, of one path, of four.

**Suits:** people drawn to method and discipline, people who like to practise and test for themselves.

## 4. Bhakti yoga: the path of devotion

Bhakti yoga turns the heart's strongest force, love, toward the divine. Instead of fighting desire, it gives desire a single object. Its practices are prayer, chanting, ritual worship, the singing of *kīrtan* and the remembering of God through the day. The Gita, whose twelfth chapter is titled *Bhakti Yoga*, says the smallest offering is enough if it's given with love:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">पत्रं पुष्पं फलं तोयं यो मे भक्त्या प्रयच्छति</p>
<p class="iast">patraṃ puṣpaṃ phalaṃ toyaṃ yo me bhaktyā prayacchati</p>
<p class="tr">A leaf, a flower, a fruit, a little water: whoever offers it to me with devotion…</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 9.26</p>
</div>

In Nepal and India this is the yoga most people actually live: the temple bell before work, the lamp lit at dusk, the pilgrimage to Pashupatinath.

**Suits:** people led by feeling, people who already pray, anyone for whom love comes easier than analysis.

## What each path changes

Swami Sarvapriyananda draws a neat distinction between them in his introduction to Vedanta. Karma yoga changes how you act, and does good while purifying the mind. Bhakti yoga changes where your desire goes, from the world to God. The path of meditation changes the state of the mind itself, focusing it until it reaches *samādhi*. Jñāna yoga is the only one that doesn't try to change anything. It aims to remove a misunderstanding about who you already are.

That's why the tradition often describes the other three as preparing the ground for knowledge, and also why most teachers insist they aren't rivals. A mind cleaned by selfless work, softened by devotion and steadied by meditation is the one best able to see clearly.

## Which one is yours?

Most people don't choose one. You might work as a karma yogi, pray as a bhakta, sit each morning in meditation and read the Upanishads at night. The paths are four descriptions of one journey, matched to four sides of human nature: understanding, hands, mind and heart.

<div class="aside">

**If you only remember one thing:** yoga has four paths: knowledge, action, meditation and devotion. Patanjali's eight limbs map the path of meditation, and posture is one step on that map. All four lead to the same freedom.

</div>
