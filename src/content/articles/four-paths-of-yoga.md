In most of the world, "yoga" means one thing: a posture class. In the tradition, it's a family of paths. The *Bhagavad Gita* teaches yoga as action, as devotion, as meditation and as knowledge, and in the 1890s Swami Vivekananda arranged these into the four paths that most Indian teachers still use. He put the idea in one sentence in the preface to *Raja Yoga*:

<blockquote>
<p>"Each soul is potentially divine. The goal is to manifest this Divinity within, by controlling nature, external and internal. Do this either by work, or worship, or psychic control, or philosophy — by one, or more, or all of these — and be free."</p>
</blockquote>

Work, worship, psychic control and philosophy: karma, bhakti, raja and jnana. Different temperaments, one goal.

## Karma yoga: the path of action

Karma yoga is doing your work fully and well, without being bound by what you get out of it. It's the Gita's first and most famous teaching:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन</p>
<p class="iast">karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana</p>
<p class="tr">Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 2.47</p>
</div>

This doesn't mean not caring about results. It means acting because the work is right, not because of the reward, and staying even when it succeeds or fails. Done this way, ordinary life becomes practice: your job, your family, service to others. Karma yoga changes how you act in the world, and through that it cleans out the selfishness in the mind.

**Suits:** active people, people with responsibilities, anyone who finds sitting still hard.

## Bhakti yoga: the path of devotion

Bhakti yoga turns the heart's strongest force, love, toward the divine. Instead of fighting desire, it gives desire a single object. Its practices are prayer, chanting, ritual worship, the singing of *kīrtan* and the remembering of God through the day. The Gita says the smallest offering is enough if it's given with love:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">पत्रं पुष्पं फलं तोयं यो मे भक्त्या प्रयच्छति</p>
<p class="iast">patraṃ puṣpaṃ phalaṃ toyaṃ yo me bhaktyā prayacchati</p>
<p class="tr">A leaf, a flower, a fruit, a little water: whoever offers it to me with devotion…</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 9.26</p>
</div>

In Nepal and India this is the yoga most people actually live: the temple bell before work, the lamp lit at dusk, the pilgrimage to Pashupatinath.

**Suits:** people led by feeling, people who already pray, anyone for whom love comes easier than analysis.

## Raja yoga: the path of meditation

Raja yoga, the "royal" yoga, works directly on the mind. Its handbook is Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras*, with its eight limbs moving from ethics and posture through breath and concentration to meditation and *samādhi*. The Gita gives its image of a settled mind:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">यथा दीपो निवातस्थो नेङ्गते सोपमा स्मृता</p>
<p class="iast">yathā dīpo nivātastho neṅgate sopamā smṛtā</p>
<p class="tr">As a lamp in a windless place does not flicker: that is the image of the yogi's controlled mind.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 6.19</p>
</div>

**Hatha yoga**, the yoga of posture and breath that modern classes come from, belongs here. The *Hatha Yoga Pradipika* says so at the start: it teaches hatha yoga for the sake of raja yoga. The body is prepared so the mind can be stilled. That's worth knowing when people say a posture class "is" yoga: in the tradition's own terms, it's the preparation for one of four paths.

**Suits:** people drawn to method and discipline, people who like to practise and test for themselves.

## Jnana yoga: the path of knowledge

Jnana yoga is inquiry. It asks the most direct question of all: what am I? Its texts are the Upanishads and the works of Vedanta, and its method is to tell apart what you are from what you merely experience, the seer from the seen. We follow one famous example step by step in [the seer and the seen](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/).

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">न हि ज्ञानेन सदृशं पवित्रमिह विद्यते</p>
<p class="iast">na hi jñānena sadṛśaṃ pavitram iha vidyate</p>
<p class="tr">There is nothing in this world as purifying as knowledge.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 4.38</p>
</div>

**Suits:** questioning, reflective people who need things to make sense before they can commit to them.

## What each path changes

Swami Sarvapriyananda draws a neat distinction between them in his introduction to Vedanta. Karma yoga changes how you act, and does good while purifying the mind. Bhakti yoga changes where your desire goes, from the world to God. Raja yoga changes the state of the mind itself, focusing it until it reaches *samādhi*. Jnana yoga is the only one that doesn't try to change anything. It aims to remove a misunderstanding about who you already are.

That's why the tradition often describes the first three as preparing the ground for the fourth, and also why most teachers insist they aren't rivals. A mind cleaned by selfless work, softened by devotion and steadied by meditation is the one best able to see clearly.

## Which one is yours?

Most people don't choose one. You might work as a karma yogi, pray as a bhakta, sit each morning as a raja yogi and read the Upanishads at night. The paths are four descriptions of one journey, matched to four sides of human nature: hands, heart, mind and understanding.

<div class="aside">

**If you only remember one thing:** posture is one preparation within one of yoga's four paths. Action, love, meditation and understanding are all yoga, and all of them lead to the same freedom.

</div>
