Most of yoga's big words, such as *samadhi*, *kaivalya* and *liberation*, sound far away. This one verse is close to home. It starts with something you're doing right now, reading this page, and in four short steps arrives at the question every yoga text is really about: who is it that's aware?

The verse opens a short text called the *Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka*, "discerning the seer from the seen". It's usually dated to around the 14th century and attributed to the Vedanta teacher Vidyaranya, though the text names no author. It's often used as a first book in Vedanta. It skips the usual opening prayers and goes straight to the point.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">रूपं दृश्यं लोचनं दृक् तद्दृश्यं दृक्तु मानसम् ।<br>दृश्या धीवृत्तयः साक्षी दृगेव न तु दृश्यते ॥</p>
<p class="iast">rūpaṃ dṛśyaṃ locanaṃ dṛk tad dṛśyaṃ dṛk tu mānasam<br>dṛśyā dhī-vṛttayaḥ sākṣī dṛg eva na tu dṛśyate</p>
<p class="tr">Form is seen; the eye is the seer. The eye is seen; the mind is the seer. The movements of the mind are seen; the witness is the seer. The witness is only ever the seer, and is never seen.</p>
<p class="ref">Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka 1</p>
</div>

## The one rule you need

The whole verse runs on a single principle, and it isn't a belief. It's common sense: **the seer and the seen are different.** Your eyes are not the page they're looking at. Whatever knows is not the thing it knows.

Two more observations come with it, and they hold at every step:

- **The seer is one, the seen are many.** One pair of eyes takes in every colour and shape in the room.
- **The seer stays relatively the same, the seen keeps changing.** The room fills, empties, gets dark and light again. The eyes looking at it are the same eyes.

Keep those three in hand and walk the verse.

## Step one: the eyes see the world

*Rūpaṃ dṛśyaṃ, locanaṃ dṛk.* Forms and colours are seen; the eye sees them. Nothing to argue with. In fact the eye can't see itself at all, only things at a distance from it. You can see a photograph of your eyes or a reflection, never your eyes directly.

## Step two: the mind sees the eyes

*Tad dṛśyaṃ, dṛk tu mānasam.* Now the eye becomes the seen. You know whether your eyes are tired, whether you need glasses, whether they're open or closed. The eyes don't know that about themselves; the mind does. The same goes for the ears, the skin, the whole body: the mind knows their changing conditions. Here "seeing" means knowing. The mind is the knower, the body the known.

The same three rules hold. The mind is different from the senses. One mind knows all of them. Their states change while the mind that knows them stays relatively the same.

## Step three: something sees the mind

*Dṛśyā dhī-vṛttayaḥ, sākṣī.* This is where it gets interesting. The mind itself is seen. You know when you understand something and when you don't. You know when you're happy, anxious, bored or remembering. You can close your eyes and look at your own thoughts. Whatever knows all this, the verse calls the **sākṣī**, the witness.

Apply the rules again. The witness is different from the thoughts it watches. Thoughts are many; the witness is one. Thoughts never stop changing; the witness doesn't change with them.

In his class on this verse, Swami Sarvapriyananda tells the story of a miserable man who climbs to a Himalayan teacher for help. The teacher asks one question: do you know you're miserable? Of course, says the man. Then, says the teacher, you're the one who knows the misery, and the knower isn't the known. The man goes away, sits with this, and comes back calm. "Now I'm peaceful," he says. The teacher's answer is the real lesson: no, you're the one who *knows* the peace. Hold on to the calm as "mine" and you'll lose it again on the way down the mountain. What you are is the knower of both.

## Step four: the seer that is never seen

*Dṛg eva, na tu dṛśyate.* The witness is only ever the seer and never becomes the seen. That's the turn that surprises most readers. The natural question after step three is "how do I see this witness?" The answer is that you can't, in the way you see a thought or a tree. If you could, it would be one more seen thing, and something else would be seeing it.

That doesn't make it unknown. Swami Vivekananda put it this way in his London lectures: the self isn't an object of knowledge, but don't conclude that it's unknown, because it is more than known. Every experience proves it. You know your eyes work not by seeing them, but because you see anything at all. In the same way, every sight, sound, thought and feeling shows not only that the experience is there, but that awareness is there.

So the eye sees the world, the mind sees the eye, the witness sees the mind, and the witness itself is never one of the things seen. In Vedanta, that witness is what you are.

## Why this belongs on a yoga site

Because Patanjali uses the same words. Right after defining yoga as the stilling of the mind's movements (1.2), the *Yoga Sutras* say what that stillness is for:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तदा द्रष्टुः स्वरूपेऽवस्थानम्</p>
<p class="iast">tadā draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam</p>
<p class="tr">Then the seer rests in its own nature.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “At that time (the time of concentration) the seer (the Purusa) rests in his own (unmodified) state.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.3</p>
</div>

And the next sutra says what happens the rest of the time: the seer takes on the shape of the mind's movements (1.4). In the second chapter, Patanjali names the root of suffering in the same terms:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">द्रष्टृदृश्ययोः संयोगो हेयहेतुः</p>
<p class="iast">draṣṭṛ-dṛśyayoḥ saṃyogo heya-hetuḥ</p>
<p class="tr">The cause of the suffering to be avoided is the conjunction of the seer and the seen.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “The cause of that which is to be avoided is the junction of the seer and the seen.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.17</p>
</div>

A few sutras later (2.20) he defines the seer as pure seeing, which looks on through the mind's ideas. Patanjali's yoga and Vedanta disagree on some big questions. Patanjali's system, built on Sāṅkhya philosophy, counts many seers and treats the world as fully real; Advaita Vedanta says there is one awareness and the world is its appearance. But on this point they agree: suffering comes from mistaking the seer for the seen, and freedom starts with telling them apart. The eight limbs, the breathwork, the meditation, all of it is there to make this one distinction clear.

That's what most yoga in the West leaves out. A class can teach you to feel calm. The tradition asks who is feeling it.

## How to read it: three levels

Swami Sarvapriyananda suggests a useful test for any verse like this, in three levels:

1. **Can I say what it says?** Not agree, not understand: just repeat it correctly. Without this, nothing else works.
2. **Do I understand it?** Can I answer my own doubts about it?
3. **Is it a fact for me?** Not a belief, not a theory to be realised after years of practice, but something I can recognise now, the way I recognise that my eyes aren't this page.

The tradition has its own version of these levels: hearing the teaching (*śravaṇa*), reflecting on it until the doubts are gone (*manana*), and dwelling in it until it's lived (*nididhyāsana*), from the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* (2.4.5).

The verse's claim is a bold one. Steps one and two are plainly facts. It says step three is too: you are already, right now, distinct from your body and mind. Not physically, but in understanding. The only thing that binds us is that we hold on.

<div class="aside">

**The monkey and the jar.** An old story, retold in the same class: a farmer catches a thieving monkey with a narrow-necked jar of bananas. The monkey reaches in and grabs one, but its fist is now too big to pull out. Nothing holds the monkey except its own grip. It only has to let go. In the swami's words, the monkey that lets go of the banana becomes a monk.

</div>

## Two honest questions

**"If I'm the witness, why does it still hurt when I burn my hand?"** Because this teaching isn't an anaesthetic. The pain is real, in the body and mind. What changes is your relationship to it: you know the pain, so you aren't only the pain. The story is told of Ramakrishna in his last illness, throat cancer making it hard to eat, admitting to a visiting disciple that it hurt terribly and, in the same breath, laughing when the disciple saw his joy. Both were true at once.

**"Isn't the witness just the mind watching itself?"** It's the right question, and Vedanta takes it seriously. Two pointers. First, when you're completely absorbed in a task, a surgeon at work or a musician playing, there's no self-reflection going on, yet you're fully conscious. Awareness doesn't depend on the mind turning to look at itself. Second, deep sleep: on waking you say "I slept well, I knew nothing." Something was there to know that nothing was known. The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* (verse 7) describes the self beyond waking, dreaming and deep sleep, and one of its names there is *śāntam*: peace. Not peaceful, but peace itself.
