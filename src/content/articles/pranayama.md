In many classes, "pranayama" means a few deep breaths before the final rest. In the yoga tradition, it's a whole limb of the path, the fourth of eight, and one the old texts treat with great care, even caution.

## What the word means

*Prāṇāyāma* joins two words. **Prāṇa** is the life force: the energy that animates the body and moves with the breath. It isn't just oxygen. When prāṇa leaves, life leaves. **Āyāma** means extension or restraint. So prāṇāyāma is the extension and regulation of the life force, worked with through the breath.

## What Patanjali says

The *Yoga Sutras* give pranayama five short sutras, right after posture:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तस्मिन्सति श्वासप्रश्वासयोर्गतिविच्छेदः प्राणायामः</p>
<p class="iast">tasmin sati śvāsa-praśvāsayor gati-vicchedaḥ prāṇāyāmaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Once posture is steady, pranayama is the regulation of the flow of inhalation and exhalation.</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.49</p>
</div>

Patanjali then describes the breath held outside, inside or simply suspended, observed by place, time and count, until it becomes **long and subtle** (2.50). Beyond these, he mentions a fourth state in which breathing pauses on its own as attention goes inward (2.51).

And he says what it does:

- It removes the covering over the inner light (2.52).
- The mind becomes fit for concentration (2.53).

That's the whole point. Pranayama comes between posture and the withdrawal of the senses because it's the bridge from the body to the mind.

## The breath and the mind

The *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, the 15th-century handbook of hatha yoga, puts the connection in one line:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">चले वाते चलं चित्तं निश्चले निश्चलं भवेत्</p>
<p class="iast">cale vāte calaṃ cittaṃ niścale niścalaṃ bhavet</p>
<p class="tr">When the breath moves, the mind moves. When the breath is still, the mind is still.</p>
<p class="ref">Hatha Yoga Pradipika 2.2</p>
</div>

You can test this now. Notice how your breath changes when you're angry, frightened or deeply focused. The breath follows the mind. Pranayama uses the reverse: steady the breath, and the mind follows.

## The classical practices

The *Hatha Yoga Pradipika* describes first purifying the energy channels (*nāḍīs*) with **alternate-nostril breathing**, then eight kinds of **kumbhaka**, breath retention, including:

- **Ujjāyī:** breathing through a slightly narrowed throat, with a soft ocean-like sound
- **Bhastrikā:** "bellows" breath, forceful and rapid
- **Bhrāmarī:** humming like a bee on the exhale
- **Śītalī:** cooling breath drawn in through the curled tongue
- **Sūrya-bhedana:** inhaling through the right nostril

For the hatha yogis, retention, not the breathing itself, was the heart of pranayama. And they took it seriously. The same text warns that done properly, pranayama removes disease, but done wrongly it causes illness, and that the breath should be tamed gradually, the way you would tame a lion or an elephant (2.15–2.16).

## How it differs from modern breathwork

Much of what's sold today as "breathwork" uses fast, deep, forceful breathing for many minutes to produce tingling, strong emotions or a sense of release. Some of it has real benefits. But it moves in the opposite direction from classical pranayama.

| | Classical pranayama | Much modern breathwork |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | slow | often fast |
| Aim | a still mind, ready for meditation | sensation, release, energy |
| Direction | toward subtle, quiet breath | toward intense experience |
| Teaching | gradual, under a teacher | often in a single workshop |

The *Bhagavad Gita* (4.29) already lists pranayama as an offering, a sacrifice: breath given up into breath. It's a practice of letting things settle, not of stirring them up.

## A safe way to begin: nāḍī śodhana

Alternate-nostril breathing, without retention, is the traditional starting point and is safe for most people.

1. **Sit comfortably** with a straight back. Rest your left hand on your knee.
2. **Raise your right hand.** Fold the index and middle fingers into the palm. You'll use the thumb for the right nostril and the ring finger for the left.
3. **Close the right nostril** with your thumb. Breathe in slowly through the left.
4. **Close the left nostril** with your ring finger, release the thumb and breathe out slowly through the right.
5. **Breathe in through the right.** Close it, open the left and breathe out through the left.
6. That's one round. **Continue for 5 to 10 minutes**, keeping the breath slow, quiet and even. Never strain.

As it gets easy, let the exhale grow a little longer than the inhale. Then simply sit for a few minutes afterwards, breathing naturally. That stillness is the point.

<div class="aside">

**Be careful with:** breath retention, rapid breathing (bhastrikā, kapālabhāti) and long holds. Learn them from an experienced teacher, and avoid them if you are pregnant or have heart, lung or blood pressure conditions. Never practise breath holding in water or while driving. If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally.

</div>
