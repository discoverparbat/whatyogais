By now the *Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka* has shown that you are the witness within ([parts 1](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/) and [2](/the-texts/mirror-of-the-mind/)) and that the world is Brahman under name and form ([parts 3](/the-texts/maya-rope-and-snake/) and [4](/the-texts/gold-and-ornaments/)). Understanding it is one thing. Living it on a crowded road is another. The text's answer is practice.

Vedanta names three stages: **śravaṇa**, hearing the teaching properly; **manana**, reflecting until every doubt is answered; and **nididhyāsana**, dwelling in it until it's simply how you see. These six meditations are *nididhyāsana*.

Swami Sarvapriyananda gives a warning first. People always want to skip ahead to the techniques. Don't. They only work if the earlier verses have been understood. Otherwise they're a pleasant relaxation exercise and nothing more.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">उपेक्ष्य नामरूपे द्वे सच्चिदानन्दतत्परः ।<br>समाधिं सर्वदा कुर्याद्धृदये वाऽथवा बहिः ॥</p>
<p class="iast">upekṣya nāma-rūpe dve sac-cid-ānanda-tatparaḥ<br>samādhiṃ sarvadā kuryād hṛdaye vātha vā bahiḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Setting aside name and form, intent on existence-consciousness-bliss, practise samādhi always, within the heart or outside.</p>
<p class="ref">Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka 22</p>
</div>

Note *sarvadā*: **always**. Not only on a cushion with eyes closed. That's the first big difference from most meditation.

## Not concentration, not mindfulness

- **Yogic meditation** holds the mind on one object: a deity, a mantra, a light. When the mind wanders, you bring it back.
- **Mindfulness** holds no object; it watches whatever arises and passes.
- **Vedantic meditation** does neither. Whatever object comes, it uses it only to turn toward **the awareness it shines in**, or **the existence it shares** with everything.

## The three inner meditations

### 1. With a thought as pointer (*dṛśyānuviddha savikalpa*)

Sit quietly. Let thoughts come: a plan, a worry, a desire, a memory. Don't push them away or follow them. Instead, notice: *this thought is shining in awareness.* The thought comes and goes; the awareness doesn't. Yesterday's thoughts were completely different; the same awareness lit them.

The swami's image: you can't see your own face, only its reflection in a mirror. But the reflection tells you, beyond doubt, that there is a face. In the same way, every thought tells you there is an awareness it's appearing in. Don't try to make that awareness an object. You can't. You *are* it.

### 2. With a Vedantic statement (*śabdānuviddha savikalpa*)

Now drop the thought, the way you'd put the mirror down once you know the face is there. Rest your attention with one statement, verse 25:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">असङ्गः सच्चिदानन्दः स्वप्रभो द्वैतवर्जितः ।<br>अस्मीति शब्दविद्धोऽयं समाधिः सविकल्पकः ॥</p>
<p class="iast">asaṅgaḥ sac-cid-ānandaḥ svaprabho dvaita-varjitaḥ<br>asmīti śabda-viddho 'yaṃ samādhiḥ savikalpakaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">"I am unattached, existence-consciousness-bliss, self-luminous, free of duality": this is samādhi with thought, joined to words.</p>
<p class="ref">Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka 25</p>
</div>

Don't chant it like a formula. See that each word is *true*:

- **Unattached** (*asaṅga*): everything you ever had came and went, every possession, every person, even this body as it was. Every night in deep sleep, the whole universe slides away, and you let it go gladly. You don't need to *practise* detachment; you already are detached.
- **Existence-consciousness-bliss** (*sac-cid-ānanda*)
- **Self-luminous** (*svaprabha*): everything is known by your light; you need no other light to know yourself
- **Without a second** (*dvaita-varjita*): nothing exists apart from it

Swami Sarvapriyananda suggests memorising this verse, at least in English. It's a lot easier to rest in when you don't have to look it up.

### 3. Without thought (*nirvikalpa*)

Then drop the words too. They were a pole-vaulter's pole: you plant it, it carries you over the bar, and you let go of it. Don't try to hold this state; it comes by itself. The text describes it with the Gita's image (6.19): a lamp in a windless place, not flickering. The mind is fully awake but has no object. What's left is what you always were.

## The three outer meditations

The same three steps, with eyes open, in the world.

### 4. With an object as pointer

Look at anything: a cup, a tree, a person. Notice: *it exists.* The cup is, the tree is, the person is. Names and forms differ; existence is the same in all of them. Attend to the existence, not the thing. Bigger things don't have more existence, only bigger names and forms.

Swami Sarvapriyananda tells of Swami Chinmayananda asking his teacher, Swami Tapovan Maharaj at Gangotri: if existence alone is real, why do we need names and forms at all? The next day, in the middle of class, the teacher asked him to fetch some water. He brought a vessel of water from the Ganges, and his teacher scolded him: I asked for water, why did you bring a vessel? Chinmayananda stood stunned. You can't bring water without a vessel. Names and forms are the vessel by which existence is experienced at all.

### 5. With a Vedantic statement

Drop the object and rest with verse 28:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">अखण्डैकरसं वस्तु सच्चिदानन्दलक्षणम् ।<br>इत्यविच्छिन्नचिन्तेयं समाधिर्मध्यमो भवेत् ॥</p>
<p class="iast">akhaṇḍaika-rasaṃ vastu sac-cid-ānanda-lakṣaṇam<br>ity avicchinna-cinteyaṃ samādhir madhyamo bhavet</p>
<p class="tr">"Reality is undivided and of one nature, marked by existence-consciousness-bliss": unbroken contemplation of this is the middle samādhi.</p>
<p class="ref">Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka 28</p>
</div>

Things are divided from one another; existence isn't. It isn't purer in a temple and dirtier in a tavern, any more than sunlight is polluted by what it shines on. The Gita chanted before meals in many Indian homes says it of a single act of offering (4.24):

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">ब्रह्मार्पणं ब्रह्म हविर्ब्रह्माग्नौ ब्रह्मणा हुतम्</p>
<p class="iast">brahmārpaṇaṃ brahma havir brahmāgnau brahmaṇā hutam</p>
<p class="tr">The offering is Brahman, the oblation is Brahman, poured by Brahman into the fire that is Brahman.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 4.24</p>
</div>

The swami tells of Swami Brahmananda, near the end of his life, being offered a drink. "Pour Brahman into Brahman," he said.

### 6. Without thought

As before, the words fall away. The text says the absorption comes with a quiet joy, *rasa*. Names and forms seem to be wiped off the world, leaving only what was always there.

## Some honest advice

- **Use whichever of the six suits the moment.** Bored of one, switch.
- **Don't mix it with a mantra practice you've been given.** Swami Sarvapriyananda is firm: if you've been initiated, keep your guru's practice and its time separate and untouched. Firewall it.
- **Don't confuse "feeling like the witness" with the witness.** Thinking "I am the witness" is a good practice, but it's a thought you produce, and it disappears in the next traffic jam. The real witness is there whether you think of it or not.
- **Mind the road.** The swami's joke: practise everywhere, but carefully on the freeway, or Brahman in the form of your car may hit some other Brahman.

## The result

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">देहाभिमाने गलिते विज्ञाते परमात्मनि ।<br>यत्र यत्र मनो याति तत्र तत्र समाधयः ॥</p>
<p class="iast">dehābhimāne galite vijñāte paramātmani<br>yatra yatra mano yāti tatra tatra samādhayaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">When identification with the body falls away and the supreme Self is known, wherever the mind goes, there is samādhi.</p>
<p class="ref">Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka 30</p>
</div>

Then, quoting the *Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad* (2.2.8), the text gives the result in one verse:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">भिद्यते हृदयग्रन्थिश्छिद्यन्ते सर्वसंशयाः ।<br>क्षीयन्ते चास्य कर्माणि तस्मिन्दृष्टे परावरे ॥</p>
<p class="iast">bhidyate hṛdaya-granthiś chidyante sarva-saṃśayāḥ<br>kṣīyante cāsya karmāṇi tasmin dṛṣṭe parāvare</p>
<p class="tr">The knot of the heart is cut, all doubts are dispelled, and all karma is exhausted, when that, the highest and the lowest, is seen.</p>
<p class="ref">Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka 31 · Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 2.2.8</p>
</div>

Practice matters, the swami says, the way medicine matters after a transplant. Without it, a new organ is never integrated, never works properly, and is eventually rejected. Without practice, an insight into the Self stays a nice idea, doesn't help in hard times, and fades.
