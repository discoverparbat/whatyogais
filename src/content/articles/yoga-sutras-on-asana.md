There are 196 sutras in Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras*, the text most often cited as the foundation of yoga. Exactly three are about posture. They don't name a single pose. Here they are, in full.

## The three sutras

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">स्थिरसुखमासनम्</p>
<p class="iast">sthira-sukham āsanam</p>
<p class="tr">Posture should be steady and easy.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Posture is that which is firm and pleasant.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.46</p>
</div>

*Āsana* literally means "seat", from a root meaning to sit. *Sthira* is steady, firm, unmoving. *Sukha* is easy, comfortable, pleasant. A good posture holds both at once: stable without strain.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">प्रयत्नशैथिल्यानन्तसमापत्तिभ्याम्</p>
<p class="iast">prayatna-śaithilya-ananta-samāpattibhyām</p>
<p class="tr">It comes through relaxing effort and resting the mind on the infinite.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “By slight effort and meditating on the unlimited (posture becomes firm and pleasant).”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.47</p>
</div>

The way to a good posture isn't more effort but less: letting go of unnecessary strain, and letting the mind settle on something boundless. That's close to the opposite of how posture is often taught, as something to push into.

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">ततो द्वन्द्वानभिघातः</p>
<p class="iast">tato dvandva-anabhighātaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">From that, one is no longer disturbed by the pairs of opposites.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Seat being conquered, the dualities do not obstruct.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.48</p>
</div>

The pairs of opposites are heat and cold, pleasure and pain, praise and blame. A steady, easy seat is the beginning of a mind that isn't thrown about by them. That's what the posture is *for*.

## What the oldest commentary adds

The earliest surviving commentary on the Sutras, the *Yoga Bhāṣya* attributed to Vyasa, gives examples of postures: the lotus seat (*padmāsana*), the hero seat (*vīrāsana*), the auspicious seat (*bhadrāsana*), the *svastika* seat, the staff (*daṇḍāsana*) and a few others.

Nearly all are **seated**. They're ways to sit still, upright and at ease, for breath work and meditation. There are no standing sequences, no inversions, no flows.

## Where the many poses came from

Later hatha yoga texts expanded the repertoire. The *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, written around the 15th century, describes fifteen postures, still mostly seated, and says tradition speaks of 84. The large number of standing poses and flowing sequences common today developed mostly in the 20th century. More on that in [is yoga just exercise?](/lost-in-translation/is-yoga-just-exercise/)

## How this can change your practice

You don't need to give up your favourite class to take Patanjali seriously. Try bringing these three sutras onto the mat:

- **Steady and easy:** in any pose, look for the point where you're stable *and* comfortable, and stay there rather than pushing past it.
- **Relax the effort:** notice what's working that doesn't need to: the jaw, the shoulders, the breath. Let it go.
- **Rest the mind:** let attention settle on the breath or on the space around you, not on how the pose looks.
- **Sit:** end every practice with a few minutes of simply sitting still. In Patanjali's terms, that's the posture.

<div class="aside">

**The test Patanjali offers** isn't how deep the pose is. It's whether you can stay in it steadily, at ease, with a quiet mind.

</div>
