Today yoga is taught in gyms from São Paulo to Seoul. Most of it is posture. Yet the first great teacher to bring yoga to the West taught almost no postures at all. How did that happen? The story runs through a handful of remarkable teachers, and at every step something was gained and something was left behind.

## 1893: a monk in Chicago

In September 1893, a young Indian monk named **Swami Vivekananda** spoke at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago. He became famous overnight. Over the next years he lectured across the United States and Britain, and in 1896 published **Raja Yoga**, a commentary on Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras*.

His yoga was about the mind: concentration, meditation, breath and the four paths of action, devotion, meditation and knowledge. He had little time for hatha yoga's physical exercises. For Vivekananda, yoga was a universal science of the spirit, which suited a Western audience curious about Eastern wisdom but wary of anything that looked like idol worship.

**Gained:** a respected, philosophical yoga, presented as compatible with modern thought.
**Left behind:** most of the devotional, ritual and physical sides of the tradition.

## 1920: Yogananda and Kriya Yoga

**Paramahansa Yogananda** arrived in Boston in 1920 and stayed in America for most of his life, founding the Self-Realization Fellowship. His *Autobiography of a Yogi* (1946) introduced millions of readers to Himalayan masters, miracles and **Kriya Yoga**, a meditation technique he taught by initiation. Again: yoga as a spiritual path, not a workout.

## The 1930s: Mysore and the birth of modern posture yoga

The posture yoga most people know grew out of the palace of Mysore in South India. There, from the 1930s, **T. Krishnamacharya** taught yoga under the patronage of the Maharaja. He combined traditional hatha yoga with flowing sequences and drew on the physical culture popular at the time, including gymnastics and wrestling exercises. Historian Mark Singleton has shown how much of today's posture practice was shaped in that period.

Krishnamacharya's students carried his yoga worldwide:

- **B.K.S. Iyengar**, whose *Light on Yoga* (1966) became the posture reference book for generations, with precise alignment and props
- **K. Pattabhi Jois**, who developed the flowing **Ashtanga Vinyasa** system, the ancestor of most "vinyasa" and "power yoga"
- **Indra Devi**, born in Latvia, the first Western woman to study with him, who opened a studio in Hollywood in 1947 and taught film stars
- **T.K.V. Desikachar**, his son, who adapted practice to the individual

**Gained:** a practical, teachable, healthy physical discipline.
**Left behind:** for many students, the reason the postures existed in the first place.

## The 1960s: the counterculture

In the 1960s yoga became part of a wider turn East. **Swami Satchidananda** opened the Woodstock festival in 1969. The Beatles went to **Maharishi Mahesh Yogi**'s ashram in Rishikesh in 1968, and his Transcendental Meditation spread worldwide. Students of **Swami Sivananda** of Rishikesh, such as Swami Vishnudevananda, set up yoga centres across North America and Europe.

For a while, yoga in the West meant mantra, meditation, vegetarianism and ashrams, as much as postures.

## The 1990s onwards: yoga as fitness

Then came the boom. Yoga moved from ashrams and community halls into gyms and studios. Styles multiplied: hot yoga, power yoga, aerial yoga, goat yoga. Yoga clothing became a fashion category. Teacher trainings became a business of their own. A 2016 study by *Yoga Journal* and Yoga Alliance counted more than 36 million practitioners in the United States alone.

**Gained:** yoga reached more people than ever before, and many found real benefit.
**Left behind:** for most, the ethics, the breath, the meditation and the goal, the seven limbs that don't fit easily into a 60-minute class.

## Why the postures won

Not because anyone planned it. Postures are:

- **Visible:** you can see a pose, photograph it and share it
- **Measurable:** you can get more flexible, stronger, better
- **Teachable in groups:** one teacher, thirty mats
- **Sellable:** classes, clothes, mats, retreats, certifications

Ethics, meditation and inquiry into the self have none of those qualities. They're slow, private and hard to package. So the market did what markets do, and rewarded the part that sells.

## What this means for you

None of this makes a posture class wrong. It's often a doorway, and many people go on to find the rest of the house. But it helps to know that what's called yoga in most of the world is one part of a much larger tradition: the outer room of a building that goes much deeper.

<div class="aside">

**If you only remember one thing:** yoga's first teachers in the West taught meditation and philosophy. The posture-centred yoga most people know took shape in the 20th century, and won out because it was the easiest part to see, teach and sell.
