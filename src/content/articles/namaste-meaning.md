In Kathmandu, a child walking to school says *namaste* to a neighbour. A shopkeeper says it to a customer. A grandson says it to his grandmother, bending a little lower. It's the most ordinary word in the world here, about as spiritual as "good morning".

Then it travelled west, and became something else.

## What the word literally means

*Namaste* joins two Sanskrit words:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">नमः + ते = नमस्ते</p>
<p class="iast">namaḥ + te = namaste</p>
<p class="tr">"a bow" + "to you" = I bow to you.</p>
<p class="ref">Sanskrit, from the root nam, to bend or bow</p>
</div>

*Namaḥ* comes from a root meaning to bend or bow. *Te* means "to you". Together, the word is simple respect: I bow to you. The more formal *namaskār* means the same, an act of bowing.

It's often said with the hands pressed together at the chest, a gesture sometimes called *añjali mudrā*, and with a small bow of the head.

## Where "the divine in me" came from

In Western yoga you'll often hear namaste translated as **"the divine in me honours the divine in you"** or "the light in me sees the light in you". It's a lovely idea, and it isn't wrong about the philosophy behind it: much of Indian thought holds that the same divine presence lives in every being, so bowing to a person can also be bowing to that presence.

But it isn't what the word *means*. It's an interpretation, popularised in the 20th century, that turns a greeting into a spiritual statement. In Nepal or India, nobody hears "namaste" and thinks of a sermon. They hear "hello, with respect".

<div class="aside">

**The difference matters** not because the poetic version is harmful, but because it quietly changes what the word is. An everyday courtesy becomes an exotic mantra, and the people who use it every day become a backdrop to someone else's spirituality.

</div>

## How namaste is actually used

- **As a greeting**, at any time of day, to anyone: friends, strangers, elders, guests.
- **With extra respect** for elders, teachers or at temples, sometimes with a deeper bow or touching an elder's feet.
- **When parting**, as well as when meeting.
- **In Nepal**, *namaste* and the more formal *namaskār* are both common; you'll hear *namaste* from mountain villages to city offices.

## Should you say namaste in a yoga class?

There's nothing wrong with it. It's a respectful greeting, and using it at the end of a class, sincerely, is perfectly fine. A few small things help it land well:

- Know it simply means "I bow to you", and say it like a greeting, not a performance.
- Use it with people, not on T-shirts and coffee mugs.
- If you travel to Nepal or India, use it everywhere. People will be delighted.
