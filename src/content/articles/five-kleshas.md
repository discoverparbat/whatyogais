Most people come to yoga for a reason: stress, pain, a restless mind, a sense that something's missing. Patanjali takes that seriously. The second chapter of the *Yoga Sutras* opens with a diagnosis of suffering itself, and it names five causes. They're called the **kleśas**, the afflictions. Swami Vivekananda translated the word as "the pain-bearing obstructions".

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">अविद्यास्मितारागद्वेषाभिनिवेशाः क्लेशाः</p>
<p class="iast">avidyāsmitā-rāga-dveṣābhiniveśāḥ kleśāḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Ignorance, egoism, attachment, aversion and clinging to life are the afflictions.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “The pain-bearing obstructions are - ignorance, egoism, attachment, aversion, and clinging to life.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.3</p>
</div>

Vivekananda calls them "the fivefold tie that binds us down". Let's take them one at a time.

## 1. Avidyā: ignorance, the root

Ignorance is the mother of the others. Patanjali says it's the field in which they all grow, whether they're asleep, weak, held down or in full flower (2.4). And he defines it precisely:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">अनित्याशुचिदुःखानात्मसु नित्यशुचिसुखात्मख्यातिरविद्या</p>
<p class="iast">anityāśuci-duḥkhānātmasu nitya-śuci-sukhātma-khyātir avidyā</p>
<p class="tr">Ignorance is seeing the impermanent as permanent, the impure as pure, the painful as pleasant, and the non-self as the self.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Ignorance is taking that which is non-eternal, impure, painful, and non-Self, for the eternal, pure, happy, Atman (Self).”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.5</p>
</div>

This isn't lack of information. A professor can be full of avidyā. It's a basic misreading of experience. Vivekananda puts the core of it in one sentence: all of us think "I am the body", and not the self, "and that is ignorance... This is the great delusion."

## 2. Asmitā: egoism

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">दृग्दर्शनशक्त्योरेकात्मतेवास्मिता</p>
<p class="iast">dṛg-darśana-śaktyor ekātmatevāsmitā</p>
<p class="tr">Egoism is taking the power of the seer and the power of seeing to be one.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Egoism is the identification of the seer with the instrument of seeing.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.6</p>
</div>

The seer is the self. The instruments of seeing are the senses, the mind, the intellect. Asmitā is confusing them: "I am the mind, I am thought; I am angry, or I am happy," as Vivekananda puts it. But how can the self be happy one moment and unhappy the next, if it doesn't change? Only through this identification.

That's exactly the confusion the first verse of the *Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka* untangles, step by step. See [the seer and the seen](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/).

## 3. Rāga: attachment

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">सुखानुशयी रागः</p>
<p class="iast">sukhānuśayī rāgaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Attachment follows pleasure.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Attachment is that which dwells on pleasure.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.7</p>
</div>

We find pleasure in something, and the mind flows toward it like a current. Next time we want it again. That pull is rāga.

## 4. Dveṣa: aversion

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">दुःखानुशयी द्वेषः</p>
<p class="iast">duḥkhānuśayī dveṣaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Aversion follows pain.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Aversion is that which dwells on pain.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.8</p>
</div>

The mirror image of attachment. Whatever has given us pain, we push away. Between them, rāga and dveṣa keep the mind swinging back and forth all day.

## 5. Abhiniveśa: clinging to life

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">स्वरसवाही विदुषोऽपि तथारूढोऽभिनिवेशः</p>
<p class="iast">svarasa-vāhī viduṣo 'pi tathārūḍho 'bhiniveśaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Clinging to life flows by its own nature and is rooted even in the wise.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Flowing through its own nature, and established even in the learned, is the clinging to life.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.9</p>
</div>

The deepest affliction: the fear of death and the grip on existence. Patanjali notes it's present even in the learned. Knowing about it doesn't remove it. Vivekananda observes it in every animal.

## How they're removed

Patanjali gives two remedies, matched to two levels. When the afflictions are subtle, seeds lying dormant, they're dissolved by tracing the mind back to its source (2.10). When they're active, as waves of thought:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">ध्यानहेयास्तद्वृत्तयः</p>
<p class="iast">dhyāna-heyās tad-vṛttayaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Their active forms are removed by meditation.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “By meditation, their modifications are to be rejected.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.11</p>
</div>

Vivekananda adds that if you keep practising meditation "for days, and months, and years, until it has become a habit", anger and hatred will be controlled and checked.

And the chapter opened with a first step for everyone, **kriyā yoga**, the yoga of action:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तपःस्वाध्यायेश्वरप्रणिधानानि क्रियायोगः</p>
<p class="iast">tapaḥ-svādhyāyeśvara-praṇidhānāni kriyā-yogaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Discipline, self-study and surrender to the Lord are kriya yoga.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Mortification, study, and surrendering fruits of work to God are called Kriya Yoga.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.1</p>
</div>

Its purpose, says the next sutra, is to bring about samādhi and to **weaken the afflictions** (2.2). To explain discipline, Vivekananda borrows the chariot from the *Katha Upanishad*: the senses are the horses, the mind the reins, the intellect the charioteer, the body the chariot and the self the rider. Discipline is simply holding the reins firmly.

## The root of the root

Later in the same chapter, Patanjali sums it all up in a single line:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">द्रष्टृदृश्ययोः संयोगो हेयहेतुः</p>
<p class="iast">draṣṭṛ-dṛśyayoḥ saṃyogo heya-hetuḥ</p>
<p class="tr">The cause of the suffering to be avoided is the conjunction of the seer and the seen.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “The cause of that which is to be avoided is the junction of the seer and the seen.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 2.17</p>
</div>

Who is the seer, Vivekananda asks? The self. What is the seen? The whole of nature, from the mind down to matter. All pleasure and pain arise from their apparent joining. The five kleshas are five faces of that one mistake.

<div class="aside">

**If you only remember one thing:** we suffer because we take what we see, the body, the mind, its moods, for what we are. Ignorance plants the seed; ego, attachment, aversion and fear grow from it. Practice and meditation pull them up by the root.

</div>
