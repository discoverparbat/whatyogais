Most spiritual stories have a hero who is old and wise. This one has a teenager. And it's not a Purana story but the frame of one of the oldest and most beautiful Upanishads, the *Kaṭha Upaniṣad*. Swami Vivekananda said he wished he had a few young people with Nachiketa's faith.

## The story

The sage Vājaśravas was performing a great sacrifice in which he was meant to give away everything he owned. But the cows he was giving to the priests were old and dry, past giving milk. His young son **Naciketā** watched, troubled. A gift like that, he felt, earns no merit at all. So he asked his father, "To whom will you give *me*?"

His father ignored him. He asked a second time, and a third. Finally his father snapped: "I give you to Death!"

Nachiketa took him at his word. He went to the house of **Yama**, the god of death, but Yama was away. The boy waited at the door for three days and three nights, without food. When Yama returned, he was dismayed: a guest, and a brahmin boy, had been left unfed in his house. To make amends he offered three boons.

**The first:** that his father's anger be calmed, and that he welcome him home. Granted.

**The second:** the knowledge of the sacred fire that leads to heaven. Yama taught it, and was so pleased with the boy's quickness that he named it after him: the *Nāciketa* fire.

**The third:** "When a person dies, some say he still exists, and some say he doesn't. Teach me this."

Yama hesitated. Even the gods have doubted this, he said. Choose something else. Take sons and grandsons who will live a hundred years. Take cattle, horses, gold, land. Take a long life. Take beautiful women and music, chariots, everything people pray for. Just don't ask about death.

Nachiketa's answer is one of the great moments in spiritual literature. All these things, he said, last only until tomorrow. They wear out the senses. Even the longest life is short. Keep your chariots, your dancing and your music. A person can't be satisfied with wealth. *This* is the boon I choose.

Yama, delighted, began to teach.

## The good and the pleasant

Yama's first lesson isn't about death at all. It's about choice:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">श्रेयश्च प्रेयश्च मनुष्यमेतस्तौ सम्परीत्य विविनक्ति धीरः ।<br>श्रेयो हि धीरोऽभि प्रेयसो वृणीते प्रेयो मन्दो योगक्षेमाद्वृणीते ॥</p>
<p class="iast">śreyaś ca preyaś ca manuṣyam etas tau samparītya vivinakti dhīraḥ<br>śreyo hi dhīro 'bhi preyaso vṛṇīte preyo mando yoga-kṣemād vṛṇīte</p>
<p class="tr">The good and the pleasant both come to a person. The wise examine them and tell them apart. The wise choose the good over the pleasant; the foolish choose the pleasant, for the sake of getting and keeping.</p>
<p class="ref">Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.2</p>
</div>

That's the whole story in one verse. Nachiketa was offered every pleasant thing there is, and chose the good.

## What Yama taught

What follows in the Upanishad is some of the clearest teaching on the Self anywhere: the Self is not born and does not die; it is not killed when the body is killed (1.2.18, echoed later in the Gita). It is subtler than the subtle, hidden in the heart, and is known not by much learning but by those it chooses to reveal itself to. Here too is the famous **chariot**: the body is the chariot, the senses the horses, the mind the reins, the intellect the driver, and the Self the rider (1.3.3–4). And the call that Vivekananda made his motto:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">उत्तिष्ठत जाग्रत प्राप्य वरान्निबोधत</p>
<p class="iast">uttiṣṭhata jāgrata prāpya varān nibodhata</p>
<p class="tr">Arise, awake, and having found the great teachers, learn.</p>
<p class="ref">Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.3.14</p>
</div>

## What the tradition reads in it

- **Śraddhā, sincere faith.** The Upanishad says *śraddhā* entered the boy when he saw the empty gifts. It's not belief, but a burning seriousness about what is true. Shankara names it as the quality that made Nachiketa fit to be taught.
- **Dispassion tested.** Yama's offers aren't cruelty. They're the test every seeker faces in some form: is it really the truth you want, or something more comfortable? See [practice and non-attachment](/beyond-the-mat/practice-and-non-attachment/).
- **Death as teacher.** The one who knows death best is the one who can teach what is deathless. Facing mortality honestly, as Nachiketa did, is where real inquiry begins.
- **The four aims of life.** Yama offers the best of *artha* and *kāma*, wealth and pleasure. Nachiketa doesn't despise them; he simply sees they end. He chooses *mokṣa*. See [the four aims of life](/what-yoga-is/four-aims-of-life/).

## If you only remember one thing

Every day the pleasant and the good stand in front of you, mixed together. The wise tell them apart, and choose the good.
