If you read only two lines of the *Yoga Sutras*, read these. The second sutra says what yoga is. The third says what happens when you get there. Everything else in Patanjali's 196 sutras, the eight limbs, the breath, the ethics, the stages of samādhi, is an explanation of how to get from one to the other.

We follow them here with the translation and commentary of **Swami Vivekananda**, from his book *Raja Yoga* (1896), the first great presentation of the *Yoga Sutras* to the West and still one of the clearest.

## 1.1: "Now"

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">अथ योगानुशासनम्</p>
<p class="iast">atha yogānuśāsanam</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Now concentration is explained.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.1</p>
</div>

*Atha*, "now", is the traditional way to begin a teaching. Commentators read it as a signal: now that you are ready, now that the world has stopped satisfying you, here is the discipline. Notice that Vivekananda translates *yoga* here simply as **concentration**. For him, that's what yoga is.

## 1.2: what yoga is

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">योगश्चित्तवृत्तिनिरोधः</p>
<p class="iast">yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Yoga is restraining the mind-stuff (Chitta) from taking various forms (Vrttis).”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.2</p>
</div>

Four words. Each one matters.

| Word | Meaning | Vivekananda's term |
|---|---|---|
| **yogaḥ** | yoga | concentration, union |
| **citta** | the whole field of the mind | "mind-stuff" |
| **vṛtti** | its turnings: thoughts, perceptions, memories, feelings | "various forms", "waves", literally "whirlpool" |
| **nirodhaḥ** | stilling, restraint, cessation | "restraining" |

### What is the chitta?

Vivekananda starts with a surprise: *the eyes do not see*. The eye is only an outer instrument. Behind it is the organ in the brain, and behind that the mind must attach itself, or you don't see at all. That's why you can sleep with your eyes open, or fail to hear carriages rolling past when your attention is elsewhere.

Then the mind passes the impression further in, to the deciding faculty (*buddhi*), and with its reaction flashes the sense of "I" (*ahaṃkāra*). Only then is the whole thing presented to the **Purusha**, the real self, who perceives it. Senses, mind, intellect and ego together are the *citta*, the mind-stuff. And the mind itself, he says, isn't intelligent. It only seems so, because the intelligent self stands behind it.

That's the same ladder as the first verse of the *Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka*: the eye sees the world, the mind sees the eye, the witness sees the mind. We follow it step by step in [the seer and the seen](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/).

### What are the vrittis?

When something from outside strikes the chitta, it reacts the way water reacts to a stone: with waves. Those waves are the **vṛttis**. Every sight, sound, thought and mood is one of them. Vivekananda's conclusion is striking: "These Vrttis are our whole universe." What we know of the world is only the mind's reaction to it.

### The lake

Then comes the image that makes the whole sutra clear. In Vivekananda's words:

<blockquote>
<p>“The bottom of the lake we cannot see, because its surface is covered with ripples. It is only possible when the ripples have subsided, and the water is calm, for us to catch a glimpse of the bottom.”</p>
</blockquote>

If the water is muddy, or always agitated, you never see the bottom. If it's clear and still, you do. "That bottom of the lake is our own true Self; the lake is the Chitta, and the waves are the Vrttis."

So yoga isn't about getting something new. It's about letting the water settle so you can see what was always there.

### Calm is not dullness

Vivekananda is careful to add that a still mind isn't a sleepy one. The mind, he says, has three states: dark and dull (*tamas*), restless and driven (*rajas*), and serene (*sattva*). Sattva is "not inactive, but rather intensely active". His line on this is one of the best in the book:

<div class="aside">

“It is the greatest manifestation of power to be calm.” Anyone can let the horses run, he says. The strong one is the one who can stop them.

</div>

He also lists the four ways the chitta shows itself: scattered, darkened, weakened and **concentrated** (*ekāgra*), and it's the concentrated mind that leads to samādhi.

## 1.3: what happens then

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">तदा द्रष्टुः स्वरूपेऽवस्थानम्</p>
<p class="iast">tadā draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam</p>
<p class="tr">Then the seer rests in its own nature.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “At that time (the time of concentration) the seer (the Purusa) rests in his own (unmodified) state.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.3</p>
</div>

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **tadā** | then, at that time |
| **draṣṭuḥ** | of the seer: the self, the Purusha |
| **svarūpe** | in its own form, its own nature |
| **avasthānam** | abiding, resting, standing |

Back to the lake. Vivekananda's comment on this sutra is two sentences: when the waves stop and the lake becomes quiet, we see the ground below; so with the mind, "when it is calm, we see what our own nature is; we do not mix ourself but remain our own selves."

That's the goal of yoga in one line. Not a feeling, not a power, not a perfect posture: the seer, no longer mistaken for what it sees, resting as itself.

## 1.4: and the rest of the time

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">वृत्तिसारूप्यमितरत्र</p>
<p class="iast">vṛtti-sārūpyam itaratra</p>
<p class="tr">Otherwise, the seer takes on the form of the mind's movements.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “At other times (other than that of concentration) the seer is identified with the modifications.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.4</p>
</div>

Vivekananda's example is ordinary and exact: someone blames me, a wave of sorrow rises, and I identify myself with it. "I am hurt." The result is misery. It isn't the wave that causes suffering. It's taking the wave for yourself.

Put 1.2, 1.3 and 1.4 together and you have Patanjali's whole diagnosis and cure: **we suffer because we identify with the movements of the mind; when they are stilled, we see that we never were them.**

## The five kinds of wave

Patanjali goes on to sort every vritti into five kinds, some painful and some not (1.5–1.11):

| Sanskrit | Vivekananda's term | In plain English |
|---|---|---|
| pramāṇa | right knowledge | perception, inference and reliable testimony |
| viparyaya | indiscrimination | mistaking one thing for another, like mother-of-pearl for silver |
| vikalpa | verbal delusion | ideas built on words with nothing real behind them |
| nidrā | sleep | the wave that holds the feeling of emptiness |
| smṛti | memory | past impressions coming back |

Even right knowledge is a wave. Yoga doesn't stop at correct thinking. It goes to the still water beneath all thinking.

## How do you still the waves?

Patanjali answers immediately:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">अभ्यासवैराग्याभ्यां तन्निरोधः</p>
<p class="iast">abhyāsa-vairāgyābhyāṃ tan-nirodhaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">They are stilled by practice and non-attachment.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Their control is by practice and non-attachment.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.12</p>
</div>

Practice is steady effort, kept up over a long time. Non-attachment is letting go of the craving that keeps throwing new stones into the lake. We look at both in [practice and non-attachment: the two wings of yoga](/beyond-the-mat/practice-and-non-attachment/). And the rest of the *Yoga Sutras*, including the eight limbs, is the detailed method.

<div class="aside">

**If you only remember one thing:** yoga is restraining the mind-stuff from taking forms (1.2). Then the seer rests in its own state (1.3). The lake settles, and you see the bottom: yourself.

</div>
