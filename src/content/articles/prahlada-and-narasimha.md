This is a story children in Nepal and India hear every year at Holi. Under it lies one of the sharpest pictures the tradition paints of the ego, and of devotion strong enough to outlast it.

## The story

The demon king **Hiraṇyakaśipu** hated Vishnu, who had killed his brother. To make himself unbeatable, he performed terrible austerities until Brahma, the creator, appeared and offered a boon. Immortality, Brahma said, was not his to give. So Hiranyakashipu asked for something almost as good. He could not be killed:

- by any man or any animal
- indoors or outdoors
- by day or by night
- on the earth or in the sky
- by any weapon

Then he declared himself lord of the three worlds and ordered that he alone be worshipped.

But his little son, **Prahlāda**, worshipped only Vishnu. Prahlada had heard of Vishnu while still in his mother's womb, from the sage Nārada, and nothing could shake him. His teachers tried to change his mind. His father pleaded, then raged. Then he tried to kill the boy: trampling elephants, venomous snakes, poison, a fall from a cliff. Prahlada came through each untouched, absorbed in remembering Vishnu. In the popular telling, the king's sister **Holikā**, who could not be burned, sat in a fire holding the boy. She burned to ash; he walked out unharmed.

Finally Hiranyakashipu, beside himself, demanded: "Where is this Vishnu of yours? Is he everywhere? Is he in this pillar?" "He is," said Prahlada. The king struck the pillar with his fist.

The pillar split open, and out came **Narasiṃha**: not a man, not an animal, but man-lion. It was twilight, neither day nor night. He seized the king, carried him to the threshold of the palace, neither indoors nor outdoors, laid him across his lap, neither earth nor sky, and killed him with his claws, which are not weapons. Every condition of the boon was kept, and every one was useless.

Even then Narasimha's fury did not settle, and none of the gods dared go near him. Only little Prahlada went up and bowed. The man-lion grew calm and offered the boy any boon. Prahlada's answer: let me never want a boon. And he asked that his father be forgiven.

## What the tradition reads in it

### Hiranyakashipu is the ego

Teachers often read the demon king as the ego, and the boon as its whole strategy. It tries to make itself safe from every possible threat: not this, not that, not here, not then. It closes every door it can think of. And once it feels secure, it wants to be worshipped. It wants to be God.

But it can't anticipate everything, because it thinks only in its own categories: man or beast, inside or outside, day or night. The divine comes from exactly the place it never imagined: a pillar, at twilight, on the threshold. The ego's armour isn't defeated by force. It's simply walked around.

### Prahlada is devotion

Prahlada doesn't fight back. He doesn't even get angry. He simply never stops remembering. That's the heart of *bhakti yoga*, the path of devotion (see [the four paths of yoga](/what-yoga-is/four-paths-of-yoga/)). In the Bhagavata, Prahlada himself lists nine forms of devotion, beginning with hearing about the Lord, singing of him and remembering him, and ending in complete self-offering.

And note his last request. Offered anything, he asks to want nothing. True devotion, the story says, isn't a deal.

### God is in the pillar

The question that ends the king's life is a mocking one: "Is he in this pillar?" Prahlada's simple yes is, in a sense, the whole of Vedanta: the divine is not elsewhere. It's the existence of every pillar, every person, every stone. See [gold and ornaments](/the-texts/gold-and-ornaments/).

## The Nepal connection

Narasimha is honoured across the Kathmandu Valley. A 17th-century stone Narasimha, tearing Hiranyakashipu apart on his lap, stands inside Hanuman Dhoka in Kathmandu's Durbar Square, and Narasimha is depicted on temples in Patan and Bhaktapur too. And every year at Holi, families light bonfires and smear colour on one another in memory of the boy who could not be burned.

## If you only remember one thing

The ego tries to make itself untouchable and wants to be God. Devotion simply keeps remembering, and the divine appears from where the ego never thought to look.
