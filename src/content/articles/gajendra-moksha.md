Of all the stories in the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*, this short one is among the most loved, and its prayer among the most recited. It's a story about the moment everyone eventually meets: when your own strength isn't enough.

## The story

On the beautiful mountain Trikūṭa there was a lake surrounded by forest. One hot day **Gajendra**, the mighty king of elephants, came down to it with his herd, his wives and his young ones. He drank, sprayed water over the little ones, played and bathed, proud and content.

Suddenly a **crocodile** seized his leg.

Gajendra pulled with all his strength, and the crocodile pulled back. The struggle went on and on, for a thousand years, the Purana says. The elephant was strong on land, but they were in the crocodile's water. Slowly his strength drained away. One by one, his herd gave up and left him.

Alone, exhausted, about to be dragged under, Gajendra stopped fighting with his strength, and turned to the Lord. He plucked a lotus from the lake, raised it in his trunk, and prayed. His prayer, the *Gajendra Stuti*, is one of the most moving hymns in the Bhagavata: not to any particular god by name, but to the one from whom everything comes, who is beyond all names and forms.

**Vishnu** came at once on **Garuda**; in some tellings he was in such haste that he leapt down and ran ahead of his own mount. With his discus, the Sudarśana chakra, he cut the crocodile's jaws open and drew the elephant out.

Both were freed in more ways than one. The crocodile had been a celestial musician, the gandharva Hūhū, cursed for a prank; Gajendra had been a king, Indradyumna, cursed for neglecting a great sage while lost in his own worship. Both were released from their curses that day.

## What the tradition reads in it

### The lake and the crocodile

The lake is **saṃsāra**, the world of pleasure and play. The elephant is enjoying himself, as we all do, strong and surrounded by family. The crocodile is what seizes us in the middle of it: death, illness, karma coming due, or attachment itself. Teachers read it different ways; all of them land on the same truth. It strikes when we're least expecting it, and in its own element we can't win.

### The herd leaves

One of the hardest details. His wives and children can't help, and eventually they go. The story doesn't blame them. It simply says what every tradition says: in the end, no one else can do this for you.

### Strength, then surrender

Gajendra doesn't pray first. He fights first, as long as he can. Only when everything he has is spent does he turn, and then his prayer is total. The tradition calls this **śaraṇāgati**, complete taking of refuge. It's the heart of *bhakti yoga*, and in Patanjali's terms it's *īśvara-praṇidhāna*, surrender to the Lord, which the *Yoga Sutras* say perfects samādhi (2.45). See [yamas and niyamas](/what-yoga-is/yamas-and-niyamas/).

The story isn't saying effort is wrong. It's saying effort has a limit, and the limit is where grace begins.

### The lotus

With nothing else to offer, Gajendra offers a single lotus. As the Gita says, a leaf, a flower, a fruit, a little water, offered with love, is enough (9.26). See [the four paths of yoga](/what-yoga-is/four-paths-of-yoga/).

### The crocodile is freed too

Even the crocodile, the "villain", is released. In the Bhagavata's world, grace doesn't only save the good. Everyone caught in the story is set free.

## The Nepal connection

The Purana sets the story on Mount Trikuta. But tradition along the **Gandaki**, the river that runs from Mustang down past our home district of Parbat as the Kali Gandaki, says it happened where the rivers meet. At **Triveni** in Nawalparasi, on the Nepal–India border, a temple complex known as Gajendra Moksha Dham marks the spot; Sonpur in Bihar, where the Gandaki joins the Ganges, makes the same claim. The Gandaki is Vishnu's river in any case: its riverbed gives the sacred śāligrāma stones worshipped as Vishnu himself.

## If you only remember one thing

Fight with everything you have. When it's not enough, and one day it won't be, let go and call out completely. That, the story says, is when help comes.
