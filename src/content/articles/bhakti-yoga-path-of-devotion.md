faqs:
  - q: "What is bhakti yoga?"
    a: "Bhakti yoga is the path of devotion: realising the divine through love of God. Instead of suppressing the mind's longing and its sense of 'mine', it turns them toward God, as 'my Lord'. Its practices include prayer, japa (repeating a divine name or mantra), chanting, ritual worship (puja) and remembering God through the day. It is one of the four yogas taught in the Bhagavad Gita, alongside jnana (knowledge), karma (selfless action) and dhyana or raja yoga (meditation)."
  - q: "Is bhakti yoga easier than jnana yoga?"
    a: "In the Bhagavad Gita (12.5), Krishna says the path to the unmanifest is harder for embodied beings. Jnana yoga only bears fruit when the student has its qualifications in a high degree: discernment, dispassion, self-control and an intense longing for freedom. Bhakti still needs those qualities, but they grow naturally out of love, the way parents' sacrifices for a child don't feel like discipline."
  - q: "What do the Narada Bhakti Sutras say?"
    a: "The Narada Bhakti Sutras are a classic text of 84 short aphorisms on devotion. Sutra 4 says that by gaining supreme love of God a person becomes perfected, immortal and fulfilled. Sutra 79 gives the whole practice in one line: at all times, in every way, free of anxiety, the Lord alone is to be worshipped."
  - q: "Why do Hindus worship God in a form if God is formless?"
    a: "Love needs something to rest on, and the mind always looks for an object. Divine names and forms give it one. Vedanta still counts them as name and form, part of Maya, but Sri Ramakrishna distinguished vidya maya, the side of Maya that frees us (divine forms, devotion, dispassion, knowledge), from avidya maya, the side that entangles us further in worldly life."
  - q: "Should I practise only bhakti yoga?"
    a: "Swami Vivekananda taught that any one of the four yogas can lead to realisation, but that practising all four together is easier, safer and more balanced, because each corrects the others' typical pitfalls. Many people make bhakti the base and practise the others in its light."

Of the four yogas, bhakti, the path of devotion, has always been the most popular. Swami Sarvapriyananda kept it for the last talk of a retreat on the [four paths of yoga](/what-yoga-is/four-paths-of-yoga/), and began with a question: if the path of knowledge is so direct, why isn't everyone enlightened?

## The fine print on the path of knowledge

Jñāna yoga makes a breathtaking offer. You already are the absolute reality; all you need is to know it. Instantaneous, effortless, direct. So where are all the enlightened people?

The answer, the swami says, is in the fine print: *terms and conditions apply.* The traditional texts list the qualifications a student of Vedanta needs:

- **Discernment** (*viveka*) between the eternal and the passing
- **Dispassion** (*vairāgya*) for the passing
- **Six disciplines**: a quiet mind settled on the teaching (*śama*), mastery of the senses (*dama*), withdrawal from too much busyness with the world (*uparati*), focus (*samādhāna*), the fortitude to keep going whatever life throws at you (*titikṣā*), and faith in the teacher and the texts, the conviction that "this is true; I don't understand it yet, but I will" (*śraddhā*)
- **An intense longing to be free** (*mumukṣutva*)

Everyone on a spiritual path has these to some degree. That's the problem: Vedanta needs them in a very high degree. Without that, people get stuck at one of several stages: no interest; interest but no understanding; understanding but no ability to live it; or a struggle so dry and steep that it wears them out. Swami Vivekananda put it plainly: on this path, many come to an understanding and few realise.

The swami gives two cases. One was a great Sanskrit scholar who had mastered Sankhya, Yoga and Vedanta. In old age he took up astrology. Asked why, he said: there's no money in Vedanta. The high point of his career, in his own telling, was being flown across India to match the horoscopes of a famous film-star couple. The other was an American nun who served for years in a hospital in Varanasi and worshipped every morning at the Vishwanath temple. One day the queue was gone and security was everywhere; she was waved through and worshipped beside a young couple. Outside, people told her excitedly: that was the most famous couple in Bollywood. She was unmoved. She had come for Vishwanath.

The scholar had the learning; the nun had the dispassion. The difference had nothing to do with understanding Vedanta.

## No right to complain

There's a saying in Uttarakhand: *who likes a weeping jñānī?* Someone who says "I am Brahman" and then complains all day. The swami remembers a fine Vedanta teacher with a fierce temper. When the monk in charge of the pantry asked who had taken an apple without asking, he exploded: was he being called a thief? Anger, he would explain afterwards, is just an object in the mind; it has nothing to do with me. Technically correct, the swami says. Not a pleasing sight.

If you claim enlightenment, you've given up your right to complain: what complaints does Brahman have? And on the path of bhakti you give up your right to be unhappy. In some Vaishnava traditions it's considered an offence to be miserable in the presence of God. Offer your problems to the Lord, then let the Lord handle them. Small temple customs, like not bowing to anyone else before the deity, not stretching your feet toward it, not eating in front of it, have the same purpose: to keep you aware of whose presence you're in. Think of the greatest person you know and how you'd behave in front of them. Now think of someone a thousand times greater.

## Why love makes it easier

Does bhakti need no qualifications, then? It needs them. But they come easily, because love is there. Arjuna asked Krishna which path was easier, and Krishna was clear:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">क्लेशोऽधिकतरस्तेषामव्यक्तासक्तचेतसाम् ।<br>अव्यक्ता हि गतिर्दुःखं देहवद्भिरवाप्यते ॥</p>
<p class="iast">kleśo 'dhikataras teṣām avyaktāsakta-cetasām<br>avyaktā hi gatir duḥkhaṃ dehavadbhir avāpyate</p>
<p class="tr">The strain is greater for those whose minds are set on the unmanifest, for the goal of the unmanifest is hard for embodied beings to reach.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhagavad Gita 12.5</p>
</div>

"Embodied" doesn't mean having a body; everyone has one, saints included. It means taking the body and mind to be what you are, as most of us do. Then understanding Vedanta is easy enough, but living by it is a struggle. That's why the Ramakrishna order recommends *jñāna-miśra bhakti*, knowledge blended with devotion, and why Vivekananda wanted all four yogas practised together.

Bhakti doesn't start by asking you to renounce the passing world. It says: keep everything in your life and add one more thing. God might be number 101 on your list, the swami jokes, just after the dog. But add God in a particular way: with *mamatā*, the sense of "mine". Not one more object, but **my** Lord, my Krishna, my Rama. The same grasping tendency that ties us to "me and mine" is turned toward God.

Then something gentle happens. A wandering monk asks you for shelter: just one corner, he needs nothing else. But he has a little image of Gopala, the child Krishna, and Gopala needs a clean corner of his own. Next morning he's picking the best flowers from your garden; the best must go to Gopala. When you go to cook, wait: Gopala's food must be offered first, and he needs a corner of the kitchen too. Little by little Gopala takes over your house. Sanskrit calls this the principle of the monk stretching his legs (*bhikṣu-pāda-prasāraṇa-nyāya*). In bhakti, it's a blessing.

The practice is still there; it just doesn't feel like practice. Parents bathe, feed and raise a child through countless difficulties, and never think "I'm practising compassion". It's love. A devotee is uneasy with the very idea of practising love for God. I love my Lord, that's all.

## The glory of bhakti

The *Nārada Bhakti Sūtras*, the classic text on devotion, describe what love of God does:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">यल्लब्ध्वा पुमान् सिद्धो भवति अमृतो भवति तृप्तो भवति ॥</p>
<p class="iast">yal labdhvā pumān siddho bhavati amṛto bhavati tṛpto bhavati</p>
<p class="tr">Gaining it, a person becomes perfected, becomes immortal, becomes fulfilled.</p>
<p class="ref">Nārada Bhakti Sūtra 4</p>
</div>

A sadhu in Uttarakhand summed it up for the swami: perfection without practice, immortality without knowledge, fulfilment without enjoyment.

- **Perfection without practice.** Normally *siddhi*, perfection, comes from *sādhana*, long practice, as in music or sport. Bhakti promises perfection simply through love of God. The practice is included; you just don't feel it as practice.
- **Immortality without knowledge.** The Upanishads say knowledge brings immortality: when you know you aren't the body, the body's death isn't yours. The sutra doesn't mention knowledge at all. Not because the devotee lacks it, but because God gives the devotee whatever knowledge is needed. Sri Ramakrishna said the Divine Mother had shown him everything in the Vedas and Vedanta.
- **Fulfilment without enjoyment.** Every advertisement promises that the next experience will fulfil you. It never does; new desires take its place, and the chase goes from thirst to thirst. The devotee is fulfilled by love of God alone.

Each path sells itself well, the swami laughs: the jñānī offers instant and effortless, the bhakta offers perfection with no practice. Neither means you can skip spiritual discipline. Bhakti is a path too.

## The name and form of God

Two great supports on this path are God's **name** and God's **form**.

Hindu worship has countless divine forms: Vishnu, Krishna, Rama, Shiva, the Goddess, Ganesha. (In California, the swami found, Ganesha is everywhere. Asked why, an American shrugged: who doesn't love an elephant?) These forms exist to attract and hold our attention, and they aren't imaginary: mystics in every age have experienced God in them.

Sri Ramakrishna once watched an early photograph being taken, on a glass plate coated with chemicals. Light passes straight through clear glass, he said; but where the glass is coated, the light leaves a picture. A purified mind is like clear glass: realisation comes as formless light. A mind coated with love comes to realisation as a vision of God in the form it loves, the way he saw Kali, the way Mirabai saw Krishna.

The swami tells a story from Srirangam, about a thousand years ago, in the lifetime of the great teacher Ramanuja. During a temple festival, with thousands following the procession, Ramanuja noticed a powerfully built young man holding an umbrella over a young woman and gazing only at her, never at the deity. The crowd was scandalised. Ramanuja sent for him. "I am Dhanurdas, a wrestler," the man said, "and she has the most beautiful eyes in the universe." Ramanuja didn't flinch. Would he like to see more beautiful eyes still? Then come to the inner shrine this evening; tell them I invited you. That night, as the priest waved the lamp before the great reclining image of Vishnu, Ramanuja took Dhanurdas's hand: look. The light fell on the Lord's face, the face came alive, and God looked back at him. Dhanurdas went into ecstasy, and Ramanuja, touching him, did too. He later married the young woman, and both became famous devotees.

Emotion needs an anchor. The pure witness, the consciousness that can never be an object, is very hard to love. Whatever you think of, that's not it. The mind always looks for an object, and divine names and forms give it one.

Doesn't that put bhakti outside Vedanta? Not an inch, the swami says. Anything that can be an object is name and form, and name and form is Maya. But Sri Ramakrishna divided Maya in two. *Avidyā māyā* entangles us ever deeper in worldly life and its dissatisfaction. *Vidyā māyā* frees us: devotion, dispassion, knowledge and the divine names and forms. They are Maya too, but focusing on them leads out.

The **name** may be the most powerful support of all. There's a story that when God divided things up at creation, God said: from now on my form is my own, but I give you my name. A senior swami of the order wrote that when you receive a mantra, you've already been given God in seed form, a direct connection. Yet we go looking on YouTube for more sophisticated techniques. Someone once asked Sri Ramakrishna for help with a spiritual problem. Clap your hands and chant the name of the Lord, he said; the birds of sin sitting in the tree of your mind will fly away. The man left disappointed. He'd wanted something advanced.

## The practice in one line

The swami's favourite sutra comes near the end of Narada's text:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">सर्वदा सर्वभावेन निश्चिन्तैर्भगवानेव भजनीयः ॥</p>
<p class="iast">sarvadā sarva-bhāvena niścintair bhagavān eva bhajanīyaḥ</p>
<p class="tr">At all times, in every way, free of all anxiety, the Lord alone is to be worshipped.</p>
<p class="ref">Nārada Bhakti Sūtra 79</p>
</div>

### Free of anxiety (*niścinta*)

A Sanskrit saying plays on two words: *cintā*, anxiety, and *citā*, the funeral pyre. Anxiety is worse than the pyre. The pyre burns a dead body; anxiety burns a living one. So surrender your worries to the Lord. Don't wait for the waves of life to settle before you turn to God: they never will. First the children's education, then their jobs, then their marriages, then the grandchildren, and the cycle starts again. Meanwhile life slips away, as Shankara sang:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">दिनयामिन्यौ सायं प्रातः शिशिरवसन्तौ पुनरायातः ।<br>कालः क्रीडति गच्छत्यायुस्तदपि न मुञ्चत्याशावायुः ॥</p>
<p class="iast">dina-yāminyau sāyaṃ prātaḥ śiśira-vasantau punar āyātaḥ<br>kālaḥ krīḍati gacchaty āyus tad api na muñcaty āśā-vāyuḥ</p>
<p class="tr">Day and night, evening and morning, winter and spring come and go again. Time plays, life ebbs away, and still the wind of hope does not let go.</p>
<p class="ref">Bhaja Govindam 12</p>
</div>

Jñāna sets strict entry conditions. Bhakti says: start now, in the middle of it all.

### At all times (*sarvadā*)

"Think of God all the time" sounds impossible, so begin small. Lock the mind down in three ways: **place, time and object** (*deśa, kāla, vastu*). In this place, my shrine or my meditation seat, I'll think of nothing but my Lord. For this half hour I'll repeat only the mantra my teacher gave me. And only this object: the luminous, loving form of my chosen deity, glowing in the lotus of the heart.

If you believe God is formless, the swami answered one questioner, then hold God's qualities instead: the all-loving, all-forgiving Lord present in your heart. An icon still helps; Eastern Orthodox Christians use them too.

The first thing you'll learn is how hard it is. Young Narendra, later Swami Vivekananda, once sang before Sri Ramakrishna, "I call on you day and night, why don't I always see you?" Sri Ramakrishna corrected him: tell the truth. You call on him once a day and once at night.

Why is it so hard? Because of impurities in the mind we often don't know are there. A young novice once insisted his mind was pure, yet he was unhappy all the time. One swami's picture of it: you lend your apartment to a friend, with a note for the caretaker, and your friend is thrown out onto the street. It's *your* apartment! It's your mind too, but put a mantra in it and the mantra is thrown out, because the mind has been taken over by everything we've invited in.

What do you do? Don't fight at the subtlest level. Move outward. If meditation isn't working, do japa; if japa isn't working, chant aloud. Offer flowers, prepare sandal paste and food, keep a shrine clean, sing. That's why ritual worship exists, even in Tibetan Buddhism, which has no creator God at all. Against those tough guys in the apartment you need some tough guys of your own: your body, your breath and your energy. The swami compares it to teaching children active reading. Stare at the page and the free part of the mind wanders off to something more interesting; underline, annotate, ask questions, and the whole mind is on the page. Puja and chanting work the same way: more of your attention is pulled into the object.

And when, deep in meditation, the form of the deity fades into a peaceful blankness? Rest a little, the swami says, then bring the form back. Otherwise the blankness passes and something else moves into its place.

From there, widen it to every moment. A sadhu told the swami: what's gone, let it go; the Lord has freed you from it. What hasn't come yet is only imagination, hope or fear. Forget the past, forget the future, and fill the present with the Lord.

### In every way (*sarva-bhāvena*)

The Upanishad says: worship the mind itself as Brahman. Advaita usually steps back from thoughts to the awareness that witnesses them, as in [The seer and the seen](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/). But the reverse is also true. If there is only one reality, every thought and feeling is made of it, as every ornament is [made of gold](/the-texts/gold-and-ornaments/) and every wave of water. Then it's my Lord alone dancing in all these thoughts, and the activity of the senses, seeing, hearing, tasting, is a performance offered to the deity shining in the heart.

Outwardly too: in the tenth chapter of the Gita, Arjuna asks how to think of Krishna while dealing with the world, and Krishna names his glories in everything. The Vedas call the sun and moon God's eyes and fire his mouth. A visitor once complained to a Himalayan monk that he couldn't bear the thought of walking on God, if the earth is God. The monk laughed. When we were little, he said, our huge uncle loved to lie on his stomach and have us children run up and down his back; it was a massage for him, and our service. Your Lord has become this earth. He's happy when his children walk on him.

## Is devotion enough on its own?

One listener pushed back: she'd grown up around very devout people who didn't seem transformed at all. The swami gave two answers. First, don't judge; it's hard enough to change ourselves. Real devotees do exist, and their presence lifts your heart. He remembered feeling a kind of holy envy near one great swami: *I wish I had that.*

Second, Swami Vivekananda's own words:

> Each soul is potentially divine. The goal is to manifest this divinity within by controlling nature, external and internal. Do this either by work, or worship, or psychic control, or philosophy, by one, or more, or all of these, and be free.

*Manifest*, the swami stresses, not just know or understand: it must become real to you, and it must show in how you think, speak and act. Each yoga alone can take you there, but Vivekananda wanted all four together. We have the power to feel, to know, to act and to concentrate; use them all. And each path has its typical pitfall, which Swami Premeshananda once spelt out for a young monk in an essay called *Yoga Vighna*, obstacles in yoga:

- **Bhakti** can slide into sentimentality and endless ritual and festivity.
- **Karma yoga** can become busyness that feels good and isn't spiritual. Premeshananda's warning used a story: a Governor-General's wife in British India longed to taste *muri*, the puffed rice ordinary people ate, so her staff emptied a tin of imported English biscuits and filled it with muri to save face. Beware, he told monks, of collecting money "for the poor" that quietly ends up housing you.
- **Raja yoga** can turn selfish: my peace, my routine, my meditation time. The real meditators, the swami noticed, would give up their meditation to nurse a sick roommate.
- **Jñāna yoga** decays into intellectualism: I've read all the books.

Practised together, Vivekananda said, they balance and correct one another. One will appeal to you more, and that's fine; it's your temperament, not a fault in the others. You can be a devotee at heart and practise all four on that basis: read the Gita because they're the words of your beloved Krishna, feed the poor because you see Krishna in them, meditate on Krishna's form.

## Find a lineage, and begin

Follow a lineage if you have one, the swami advised, and stay open to learning from others. If you need a new one, make sure it really is a lineage. Would you buy a computer from a man outside the Apple store who built it in his garage, cheaper and better? Maybe it is. But there's no record, no satisfied customers, no "garage care". With the biggest investment of your life, choose a path that others have walked for centuries, with texts you can check.

But don't be too choosy either. He meets seekers in the Himalayas who are still searching for the perfect guru after twenty years. Somewhere inside, the swami suggests, is the thought that no one else is good enough. Sri Ramakrishna said that someone truly thirsty will brush the scum off a puddle and drink. Lineage matters. But don't delay: start.

He ended with a question to ask yourself. Of all the thoughts in your mind, which is more valuable than the thought of God and your mantra? Apart from a few urgent tasks, almost none. Thieves toss a coin on the ground and pick your pocket while you bend for it. Farmers scatter a little sugar so the mice ignore the grain store all night. The world does the same with temptation, fear and anxiety, and while we chase them, the thought of God is gone. Gently pull the mind back, and give it to the name of God.
