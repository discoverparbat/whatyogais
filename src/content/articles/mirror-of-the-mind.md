In [part 1](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/) the *Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka* showed, in four steps, that you are not the body or the mind but the witness of both. Fair enough on paper. But a question should come straight away, and Swami Sarvapriyananda says it's the right one to ask: if I'm the witness, why does my body feel so obviously *me*?

A microphone is an object of experience, and so is my hand. But I never say "I am a microphone". I do say "I am tall", "I am tired", "I am hungry". Pinch my arm and I feel it. The body is lit up with consciousness right to the fingertips. Where does that come from?

## Three layers of you

Indian philosophy doesn't divide a person into just body and mind. It sees three layers:

- **The gross body** (*sthūla śarīra*): flesh, bone, the body that weighs 60 kilos
- **The subtle body** (*sūkṣma śarīra*): the life-force that breathes and digests (*prāṇamaya kośa*), the mind with its thoughts, memories and sense of "I" (*manomaya kośa*), and the intellect that understands and decides (*vijñānamaya kośa*)
- **The Self** (*ātman*): pure consciousness, the witness, which is not a body at all

The English word "soul" blurs the last two. In Vedanta they must be kept apart.

## The sun in the water

When the sun shines, every surface reflects some light: walls, rocks, trees. But a few surfaces, like a pool of water, a polished plate or a dewdrop, do more. They form a little image of the sun.

The subtle body is that kind of surface. Pure consciousness shines on it, and an image of consciousness forms there. Vedanta calls it ***cidābhāsa***, reflected consciousness. That reflection is exactly the awareness you feel right now, reading this. Nothing mystical: it's your ordinary sense of being aware.

Swami Sarvapriyananda adds the moon. At night the moon lights up the world, and for practical purposes it gives light. But it has none of its own. It borrows from a sun you can't see. The mind is like the moon: bright, useful, and borrowing every bit of its light.

### Borrowed and intrinsic

How do you know the body's consciousness is borrowed? Because it comes and goes. It's gone in deep sleep, in fainting, at death. His kitchen example makes it plain. Vegetables in a pot are hot, but take them off the stove and they go cold: their heat was borrowed from the water. The water borrowed it from the pan, the pan from the fire. Only the fire is hot on its own account.

In the same way: the body borrows consciousness from the senses, the senses from the mind, the mind from reflected consciousness, and that reflection from the witness. **The witness alone is conscious on its own account.**

## The red-hot iron ball

Verses 6 and 7 go one step finer. Inside the mind, isolate one thing: the sense of "I", the ego (*ahaṅkāra*). The text says the ego and reflected consciousness become fused, like a red-hot iron ball.

Look at such a ball fresh from the furnace. It seems to be one thing: round, heavy, glowing, hot. But two things are mixed. The roundness and weight belong to the iron. The glow and heat belong to the fire. The ego is the iron; reflected consciousness is the fire. Fused together, the "I" glows with awareness, and passes that glow to the rest of the mind, the senses and the body. That's why the body feels conscious, and why we say "I am this body".

## The three relationships of the "I"

Verse 8 then asks the most practical question in the text: how is this "I" related to three things around it?

| The "I" and… | Relationship | Can it be cut? |
|---|---|---|
| reflected consciousness | **natural** (*sahaja*), like a mirror and its reflection | only when the mind shuts down, in deep sleep |
| the body | **born of karma** (*karmaja*) | only when that karma runs out, at death |
| the witness | **born of error** (*bhrāntija*) | **yes, by knowledge** |

The first two you can do nothing about while you live. But the third isn't a real relationship at all. It's a mistake, like seeing a snake in a rope. You don't get rid of that snake with a stick or a gun. You get rid of it by seeing the rope. That's the whole work of Vedanta, and it's why the swami says it is not the ego that becomes free. **You become free from the ego.**

## Waking, dreaming, sleeping: states of the mind

If consciousness never changes, why do I feel bright in the morning, dull at night, strange in dreams and absent in deep sleep? Verses 10 and 11 answer: those are states of the *mind*, not of consciousness. A polished mirror gives a sharp reflection, a foggy one a dim reflection, a mirror turned face down none at all. The face itself never changes.

Swami Sarvapriyananda tells it with a station master on a railway platform. In the morning the big express pulls in: crowds, noise, deals, deadlines. That's waking. It leaves, and a slow goods train comes in, half lit, a few shapes moving about. That's dreaming. It leaves too, and the platform is empty. That's deep sleep. Through all of it the station master stands watching. He didn't arrive with any train and he doesn't leave with one. The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* builds its whole answer to "who am I?" on these three states; see [Who am I? Waking, dreaming, deep sleep and the fourth](/the-texts/who-am-i-mandukya-upanishad/).

## Birth and death

Verse 12 deals with birth and death in one stroke. The subtle body enters a new physical body: that's birth. It leaves a worn-out one: that's death. The Gita's image is changing clothes (2.22). Pure consciousness does neither.

The swami's picture: a garden full of buckets of water, each with a little sun shining in it. If the little sun could think, it would say "I am this bucket". Vedanta tells it: you're not the bucket (the gross body), not the water (the subtle body), not even the little reflected sun. **Look up.** When it does, it doesn't see the sun. It finds it *is* the sun, looking down at its reflections in every bucket at once.

## Why this is yoga

Patanjali says that outside of yoga, the seer takes on the form of the mind's movements (*Yoga Sutras* 1.4). This section of the *Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka* shows exactly how that happens: consciousness reflected in the mind, fused with the "I", spread through the body. Yoga, in this light, is the slow undoing of that one error.
