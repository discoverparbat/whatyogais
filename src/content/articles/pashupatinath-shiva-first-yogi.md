Before Patanjali, before the Gita, yoga's own tradition names a first teacher. It isn't a historical person. It's Shiva: the great ascetic, ash-smeared, seated in meditation on the snows of the Himalaya. And in Kathmandu, on the banks of the Bagmati river, stands the temple that for many Hindus is his holiest home: **Pashupatinath**.

## Ādinātha: the first lord of yoga

The *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, the classic handbook of hatha yoga, opens not with a technique but with a bow:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">श्रीआदिनाथाय नमोऽस्तु तस्मै येनोपदिष्टा हठयोगविद्या</p>
<p class="iast">śrī-ādināthāya namo 'stu tasmai yenopadiṣṭā haṭha-yoga-vidyā</p>
<p class="tr">Salutations to the glorious Ādinātha, who taught the science of hatha yoga.</p>
<p class="ref">Hatha Yoga Pradipika 1.1</p>
</div>

**Ādinātha**, "the first lord", is Shiva. For the Nath yogis, whose lineage runs through Matsyendranath and Gorakhnath, Shiva is the source of the whole teaching: he taught it to Parvati, and Matsyendranath, in the form of a fish, overheard. The Naths take their very name, *nātha*, "lord", from him.

The image of Shiva the yogi is much older than hatha yoga. The **Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad**, a text devoted to Shiva as Rudra, contains one of the earliest descriptions of yoga practice in its second chapter: sit with the body upright, control the breath, restrain the mind as you would a team of wild horses, in a quiet, clean place sheltered from the wind.

## The Pashupati seal: an ancient question

In the 1920s, archaeologists in the Indus Valley city of Mohenjo-daro found a small stone seal, over 4,000 years old, showing a horned figure seated with legs folded, surrounded by animals. Sir John Marshall called it a **"proto-Shiva"**, an early form of Pashupati, lord of animals, in a yogic posture.

It's a beautiful idea, but honesty matters here: scholars still debate it. The posture may or may not be yogic, the figure may or may not be related to Shiva, and the Indus script hasn't been deciphered. It's best seen as an open question, not as proof that yoga is 5,000 years old.

## What "Pashupati" means

*Paśu* means a creature, an animal. *Pati* means lord. So **Paśupati** is the lord of all creatures, and Paśupatinātha adds *nātha*, protector.

In Shaiva philosophy the words carry a deeper meaning, and it's the language of yoga:

- **Paśu:** the individual soul, bound like a tethered animal
- **Pāśa:** the bonds that tie it: ignorance, action and its results, the impurities of the mind
- **Pati:** the Lord who frees the soul from its bonds

The **Pāśupata** sect, one of the oldest Shaiva ascetic movements, took its name from this idea. Pashupati isn't just a protector of animals. He's the one who unties us, the liberator. That's exactly what every yoga text says it's for.

## The temple on the Bagmati

Pashupatinath stands on the western bank of the Bagmati river, on the eastern edge of Kathmandu. Its history goes back at least to the Licchavi kings of the first millennium CE, and its legends much further. The present golden-roofed pagoda dates from the late 17th century. In 1979 it became part of the Kathmandu Valley UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Inside the sanctum is a **mukhaliṅga**, a stone liṅga carved with faces of Shiva looking in the four directions. Only Hindus may enter the main courtyard. By long tradition, the main priests (*bhaṭṭas*) come from South India, a custom often traced to Shankaracharya.

For anyone, Hindu or not, the wider complex is extraordinary:

- **The ghats.** Along the river, the open-air cremation platforms burn day and night. It's the most direct teaching on impermanence you'll ever see, and the reason many Hindus hope to be cremated here.
- **The sadhus.** Renunciants in saffron and ash, many of them Shaiva yogis, live and gather around the temple. Some are serious practitioners. Some pose for photographs for money. Telling them apart takes time and respect.
- **The evening aarti.** Every evening, priests on the riverbank offer lamps, incense and song to the Bagmati and to Shiva as the light fades.
- **The hillside.** Across the river, the forested Shleshmantak hill holds dozens of small shrines and lingas, quieter and full of monkeys.

## Maha Shivaratri

Once a year, on **Mahā Śivarātri**, the great night of Shiva, usually in February or March, Pashupatinath fills with sadhus from across Nepal and India, and with hundreds of thousands of pilgrims. Fires burn all night. Many devotees fast and stay awake, keeping vigil with Shiva the meditator. If you want to see the living yoga tradition of the Himalaya in one place, this is the night.

## Why it matters for yoga

In most of the world, yoga has become something you do in a studio. At Pashupatinath you see where it came from: a tradition of renunciants, mantra and fire, seated stillness and an unflinching look at death, gathered around the image of a god who is, above all, a yogi.

You don't have to share that faith to learn from it. But you'll understand yoga better for having seen it.

<div class="aside">

**Visiting respectfully:** dress modestly, cover shoulders and knees, and don't photograph cremations up close or without permission. Ask before photographing sadhus, and if you do, a small offering is customary. Remember that for families on the ghats this is a funeral, not a sight.
