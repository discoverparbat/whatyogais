It's one of the most asked questions about yoga, and one of the most argued about. Some Christian and Muslim leaders have warned their communities away from it as Hindu worship. Some Hindus object that the West has taken yoga and stripped out its spiritual roots. Meanwhile millions of people stretch on a mat once a week and never think about it at all.

The honest answer is that **yoga is deeply spiritual, comes out of Indian religious traditions, and yet isn't a religion in the way the word is usually meant in English.** Each part of that sentence matters.

## Why "religion" fits badly

The English word *religion* usually means a defined faith: a creed you believe, a community you join, sometimes a conversion. Sanskrit has no exact match for it. The nearest words are:

- **Dharma:** the order of things, and the right way of living within it
- **Darśana:** a "way of seeing", one of the classical schools of philosophy

Yoga is traditionally counted as one of the **six darśanas** of Indian philosophy, alongside its sister school Sāṅkhya, and Vedānta, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika and Mīmāṃsā. It's a philosophy with a method: a description of the mind and a set of practices to free it.

## The case that yoga is religious

It would be dishonest to pretend yoga is just exercise with Sanskrit names. Consider:

- **Its goal is spiritual.** Patanjali's aim is *kaivalya*, liberation. The Gita's is union with the divine. That's not fitness.
- **Its texts are scripture.** The *Bhagavad Gita* is one of Hinduism's most sacred books. The Upanishads, which describe yoga early on, are part of the Veda.
- **Its practices include devotion.** Chanting Om, mantra, worship of a chosen deity and surrender to the Lord are all part of the tradition.
- **Its teachers were religious figures.** From the Nath yogis to Swami Vivekananda, yoga was taught by monks and renunciants within living Hindu traditions.

Many Hindus, including the Hindu American Foundation's 2010 "Take Back Yoga" campaign, have asked that yoga's Hindu roots be acknowledged rather than erased. That's a fair request.

## The case that it isn't a religion

- **It asks for no creed.** Nothing in the *Yoga Sutras* requires you to accept a scripture, join a community or convert.
- **It's shared across traditions.** Buddhist and Jain traditions developed their own yoga and meditation. The word *yoga* appears in Buddhist texts too.
- **Its God is left open.** That's the most striking point, and it's worth looking at closely.

## What Patanjali actually says about God

The *Yoga Sutras* do mention a Lord, **Īśvara**, and recommend devotion to him:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">ईश्वरप्रणिधानाद्वा</p>
<p class="iast">īśvara-praṇidhānād vā</p>
<p class="tr">Or [samādhi comes] through devotion to the Lord.</p>
<p class="viv">Swami Vivekananda: “Or by devotion to Isvara.”</p>
<p class="ref">Yoga Sutras 1.23</p>
</div>

Notice the small word *vā*: **or**. Devotion is offered as one way among others, not a requirement. And Patanjali's definition of Īśvara is remarkably general: a special being untouched by afflictions, actions and their results (1.24), the teacher of even the ancient teachers, unlimited by time (1.26), whose word is **Om** (1.27). No name, no myth, no temple.

Classical commentators read this in different ways. Some saw a full theistic system; others noted that Sāṅkhya, the philosophy yoga is built on, needs no creator at all. Either way, the text leaves room. That's why Christians, Muslims, Jews, Buddhists and atheists have all found a place to practise.

## What courts and governments have said

The question has reached courtrooms. In 2015, in *Sedlock v. Baird*, a California appeals court ruled that a public-school yoga programme in Encinitas, taught without Sanskrit names, chanting or spiritual teaching, was not religious instruction. In 2014 the United Nations declared 21 June the **International Day of Yoga**, describing yoga as a practice for health and well-being for all.

Those rulings are about yoga *as taught in a school gym*. They say a stripped-down version isn't religious. They don't say the tradition itself has no spiritual content.

## So: is it?

Yoga is best understood as **a spiritual discipline, not a religion**. It grew in the soil of Hindu, Buddhist and Jain India, and pretending otherwise is both inaccurate and disrespectful. But it doesn't ask you to change your faith. It asks you to change your mind, in the most literal sense: to still it.

A Christian can practise ahiṃsā and truthfulness. A Muslim can practise breath and concentration. An atheist can sit and watch thoughts come and go. What the tradition asks is honesty about where the practice comes from, and respect for the people for whom it's still sacred.

<div class="aside">

**If you only remember one thing:** yoga comes from religious traditions and has a spiritual goal, but it requires no belief, no conversion and no particular god. You can honour its roots without adopting a religion.

</div>
