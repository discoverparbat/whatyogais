By verse 31 the *Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka* has said what it came to say. But it adds fifteen more verses, almost a postscript, to answer the question every thoughtful student eventually asks. Swami Sarvapriyananda says people wrote to him about it from all over the world:

**If I am Brahman, then who is the one in ignorance?** Brahman doesn't need Vedanta classes. Brahman isn't seeking liberation. Who is?

Shankara was once asked exactly this, the swami says. His answer: why do you ask? "Because I don't know." Then you are the one in ignorance. This teaching is for you.

## Three selves

The text's real answer is a map. Think of yourself in three ways (verse 32):

| Self | What it is | Level of reality |
|---|---|---|
| **The witness** (*pāramārthika jīva*) | pure consciousness, the seer of the mind | absolute |
| **The waking self** (*vyāvahārika jīva*) | consciousness reflected in the mind: the person you take yourself to be right now | empirical, transactional |
| **The dream self** (*prātibhāsika jīva*) | the "you" in tonight's dream | apparent |

Swami Vivekananda called the first two "the real man and the apparent man".

## Three levels of reality

Advaita says only Brahman is real. But we don't live there. So it gives the world we do live in a provisional reality, and describes three levels:

- **Pāramārthika**, absolute: Brahman, existence-consciousness-bliss
- **Vyāvahārika**, empirical: this waking world of work, family, roads and taxes. Real enough to act in
- **Prātibhāsika**, apparent: dreams, mirages, the snake in the rope. Seen as false even from the waking world

The key rule: **each lower level resolves into the one above it.** The dream snake resolves into the waking rope; you wake and say "it was only a dream". Enlightenment is the same move one level up: the whole waking world, body and mind included, is recognised as Brahman.

## The limit isn't real

The text has called the witness "consciousness limited by the mind". Doesn't that make it limited? Verse 33 answers: the limiter is imaginary, a name and form; the limited is real. An imaginary thing can't actually limit a real one. The commentary's image: the water of a mirage, however big the lake looks, cannot wet a single grain of desert sand.

Swami Sarvapriyananda uses a glass of water. Move the glass: the water moves with it. Does the space inside the glass move too? It seems to, but really the glass moves through space. Space was never inside it.

## Which "you" is Brahman?

This finally makes the Upanishads' great statements precise (verse 34). Each of the four Vedas has one:

- **तत्त्वमसि** *tat tvam asi*: you are that (*Chāndogya Upaniṣad*)
- **अहं ब्रह्मास्मि** *ahaṃ brahmāsmi*: I am Brahman (*Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad*)
- **प्रज्ञानं ब्रह्म** *prajñānaṃ brahma*: consciousness is Brahman (*Aitareya Upaniṣad*)
- **अयमात्मा ब्रह्म** *ayam ātmā brahma*: this self is Brahman (*Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*)

Which "you"? Not the dreamer. Not even the person reading this, with a history and plans. **The witness of that person.** And the identity runs both ways: you are nothing other than God, and God is nothing other than the real you. Not "a part of" or "a servant of". The same.

## So who is bound?

The waking self (verses 35–41). Maya veils the witness and projects it as an individual: consciousness reflected in a mind. Whatever happens to the mirror now seems to happen to the face. The mind is sad: "I am sad". The body is old: "I am old". Acting from desire, it becomes a doer and an experiencer, earns results good and bad, and moves from life to life. That process has no beginning, since time itself is part of Maya, but it **has an end**: knowledge.

And notice what freedom means here. Swami Sarvapriyananda insists: the person doesn't become free. **You become free from the person.** Mr or Mrs So-and-so doesn't become "Mr Brahman". The individuality is seen for what it was, a reflection, while the body and mind carry on as before.

## Water, wave and foam

The text closes (verses 42–46) with one of the loveliest images in Vedanta. Still water is whipped into a wave; on the wave's crest forms foam.

- **Foam** is the dream self
- **The wave** is the waking self
- **The water** is the witness, Brahman

Water's coolness, fluidity and taste are its own. The wave only borrows them, and the foam borrows them from the wave. When the foam settles, its qualities return to the wave; when the wave settles, to the water. And at every moment it was all water.

In the same way, the existence, awareness and joy you feel now, and the ones you feel in tonight's dream, are borrowed from Brahman. You don't *become* Brahman later. You are that now. Enlightenment is only knowing it.

## What does the enlightened person see?

Swami Sarvapriyananda tells of being a young novice at Belur Math, standing outside the crowded room of Swami Bhuteshananda, then president of the Ramakrishna Order and in his late nineties. A senior monk asked: we see the enlightened person eating, talking and seeing differences, just like us. The old swami answered: that's what *you* see. Then what does the enlightened person see? Instantly, the reply came: **who sees?**

And why do the enlightened stay with us at all? Ramakrishna's answer was a story. Three friends climb a high wall to see what's on the other side. The first reaches the top, dances with joy and jumps over. So does the second. The third climbs up, sees a great festival of joy below, and is about to jump. Then he thinks of the people back in the village, turns around, and climbs down to tell them.

That, the swami says, is what a teacher is.
