Ask what yoga is *for*, and most answers you'll hear today are about the body and the mind: flexibility, strength, less stress, better sleep. All good things. But the tradition yoga comes from answers differently. Yoga is the art of using this human body, and this human life, as a tool for one thing: **realisation**.

To see why, you need the framework every Indian child once grew up with: the four aims of life.

## The four aims: puruṣārthas

Classical Indian thought names four things a human being rightly pursues, the *puruṣārthas*, "aims of a person":

| Aim | Meaning | Its place |
|---|---|---|
| **धर्म dharma** | right living: duty, ethics, the order that holds life together | **the foundation** |
| **अर्थ artha** | wealth, livelihood, security, the means of life | pursued on that foundation |
| **काम kāma** | pleasure, love, beauty, enjoyment | pursued on that foundation |
| **मोक्ष mokṣa** | liberation: realising what you really are | **the goal** |

This isn't a world-denying list. Artha and kāma are honoured. Earning well and enjoying life are part of a full human life, and the tradition produced whole sciences of both. The point is their **order**.

## Dharma first

Wealth and pleasure pursued without dharma destroy people. Pursued within it, they flourish. The *Mahābhārata*, at its very end, has its author Vyāsa cry out:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">ऊर्ध्वबाहुर्विरौम्येष न च कश्चिच्छृणोति मे ।<br>धर्मादर्थश्च कामश्च स धर्मः किं न सेव्यते ॥</p>
<p class="iast">ūrdhva-bāhur viraumy eṣa na ca kaścic chṛṇoti me<br>dharmād arthaś ca kāmaś ca sa dharmaḥ kiṃ na sevyate</p>
<p class="tr">With arms raised I cry out, and no one listens: from dharma come wealth and pleasure. Why, then, is dharma not followed?</p>
<p class="ref">Mahābhārata, closing verses</p>
</div>

In yoga, dharma has a precise form: the **yamas and niyamas**, the first two of Patanjali's eight limbs. Non-harm, truthfulness, not stealing, restraint, not grasping; cleanliness, contentment, discipline, study, surrender. See [yamas and niyamas](/what-yoga-is/yamas-and-niyamas/). And in the Gita's **karma yoga**, dharma becomes a way of working: do your own duty fully, without clinging to its fruits (2.47).

## Moksha as the goal

Artha and kāma, however well pursued, end. Every gain is lost, every pleasure passes, and the body that enjoys them grows old. Swami Sarvapriyananda tells it with an airline story: the comfortable seat, the film, the meals at forty thousand feet are pleasant. Then the plane lands, and you're asked to leave. Even heaven, in the Indian traditions, has an expiry date.

That's why the fourth aim sits above the rest. **Mokṣa**, liberation, isn't one more thing to get. It's realising what you already are: the seer, pure awareness, one with Brahman. See [what is yoga, really?](/what-yoga-is/what-is-yoga/) and [the seer and the seen](/the-texts/seer-and-the-seen/).

## The human body as the instrument

Here is where yoga's view of the body becomes clear. The body isn't the goal, and it isn't the enemy. It's the **instrument**. Kalidasa's line became a proverb across India:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">शरीरमाद्यं खलु धर्मसाधनम्</p>
<p class="iast">śarīram ādyaṃ khalu dharma-sādhanam</p>
<p class="tr">The body is, indeed, the first instrument of dharma.</p>
<p class="ref">Kālidāsa, Kumārasambhava 5.33</p>
</div>

And the *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi*, attributed to Shankara, opens its teaching with a reminder of how rare that instrument is:

<div class="verse">
<p class="dev" lang="sa">जन्तूनां नरजन्म दुर्लभम्</p>
<p class="iast">jantūnāṃ nara-janma durlabham</p>
<p class="tr">Among all creatures, a human birth is hard to obtain.</p>
<p class="ref">Vivekacūḍāmaṇi 2</p>
</div>

Why rare? Because only a human being can turn and ask *who am I?*, and find the answer. That's what the body is for. Look after it, strengthen it, keep it healthy: hatha yoga's postures and breath exist for exactly this. But as the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika* says itself, all of it serves the higher yoga of the mind. A tool kept sharp is a tool kept for a purpose.

## Where modern yoga stops short

Seen through the four aims, much modern yoga lives entirely in artha and kāma: a better body, a calmer mind for a more productive week, a pleasant hour. None of that is wrong. Dharma permits it. But the tradition would call it a beginning mistaken for the end, like keeping a boat polished and never crossing the river.

The full picture is simple:

- **Live by dharma.** Ethics first, in work, family and every dealing.
- **Pursue artha and kāma within it.** Earn, love, enjoy, without guilt and without being ruled by them.
- **Keep mokṣa as the goal.** Use this body and this life, while you have them, for realisation.

<div class="aside">

**If you only remember one thing:** yoga is the use of this precious human body and life as an instrument for realisation. Dharma is the ground you build on, wealth and pleasure are welcome guests, and moksha is the goal.

</div>
